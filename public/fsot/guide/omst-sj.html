OMST: situational judgment

OMST · 3 min read

Situational judgment items give you a workplace scene and ask for two answers, not one. State's specialist assessment guide puts it plainly: the section asks candidates to supply the best answer choice and the worst answer choice from the options presented. The FSOT ran its own version the same way until it was retired in October 2025, and presented each scenario twice, once for the best pick and once for the worst.

Take the worst pick first. It separates more cleanly, because a clearly wrong answer is easier to recognise than a best answer competing with a good one, and eliminating it shortens the field for the pick that is harder.

Four questions do the separating, and all four are visible in the scenarios State has published. Does the option break a rule? Does it act on the problem, or work around it? Does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it? Is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect?

Watch those four work on a published item. A friend asks you to approve a non-standard procurement quickly; policy says it needs a fuller evaluation. Expediting it as a favour breaks the rule outright, which settles the worst pick before anything else is weighed. Two options hold the rule: telling the officer that friendship aside you follow policy, and telling him you will move as fast as you can but it still gets vetted. Both are defensible. The second also keeps the working relationship, and that is usually what separates a correct answer from the best one.

One line from State's own practice instructions outranks any heuristic: knowledge about the Department's policies, procedures, or organizational culture is not required to answer these questions. The scenarios are set in an embassy, but they are not testing whether you have read the Foreign Affairs Manual. Read them as ordinary problems between working adults.

State has published ten of these scenarios and an answer key for none of them. Everything above is reasoning rather than a key, and it is the reasoning that carries into the test. Where an item has two defensible readings, being able to say why one is defensible is the skill being scored.

Drill

Tap the four questions that separate the options, in the order you ask them.

Show the full sequence
  1. Does it break a rule?
  2. Does it act on the problem, or work around it?
  3. Does it go to the nearest person who can fix it?
  4. Is it proportionate to what you know?
