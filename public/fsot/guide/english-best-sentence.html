English: choosing the best sentence

Math, Logic & English · 2 min read

Four sentences, almost identical, one correct. Your ear will solve some of these and will quietly lose you the rest, because the errors that survive into this format are the ones nobody hears. A missing comma after a year is silent. That where the sentence wants which is silent. Who standing in for whom is silent. All four options read cleanly aloud, so reading them aloud tells you nothing.

Stop reading for the best-sounding option and start diffing. Put the four side by side and find the places where they differ — there are usually only one or two, and often a single word. Everything the options agree on is not being tested and can be ignored entirely, however long the sentence is.

Name the rule at each difference, one at a time. If the options vary a comma around however, that is the rule for an interrupting adverb. If they vary that and which, that is restrictive against non-restrictive. If they vary who and whom, work out whether the pronoun is doing the verb or receiving it. Settle each difference on its own before you look at any option as a whole.

Then the trap, which is the whole reason this format is hard. When two things vary, an option can be right about the one you noticed and wrong about the one you did not. A sentence that correctly fences however in commas and then drops the comma after the year is built to catch someone who checked the first thing and stopped. Having found one difference, look for a second before you answer.

Some things sound wrong and are: should of for should have, went for gone, a plural subject with a singular verb. Take those quickly — they are the items your ear can win. Spend the time you save on the silent ones.

Four silent errors worth recognising on sight. A month-day-year date inside a sentence closes with a comma after the year, not just between day and year. A semicolon needs a full sentence on both sides, so one standing before a phrase beginning with a participle is wrong however balanced it looks. Correlatives take matched halves, so both a foot in its future and a foot in its past, never both a foot in its future and in its past. And an identifying clause takes that with no comma, while an aside takes which with one.

Drill

Tap the four steps for a best-sentence item, in order.

Show the full sequence
  1. Diff the options and mark every place they differ
  2. Ignore everything all four agree on
  3. Name the rule at each difference and settle it alone
  4. Check for a second difference before answering
