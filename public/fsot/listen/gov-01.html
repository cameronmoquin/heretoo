Transcript

Episode seven: the Constitution's architecture. Review first; answer out loud in the pauses. From the logic drill: what's the test for a necessary assumption? Negate it; if the argument dies, that was the assumption. And the most tested confusion in logical reasoning? Necessary versus sufficient. Keep that one warm, because it's also the key to constitutional questions: what government may do versus what it must do. Three questions this episode will answer. One: what specific failures of the Articles of Confederation does each major constitutional power answer? Two: what deals were cut at Philadelphia, and who got what? Three: how does an amendment actually happen, and why has it only happened twenty-seven times? Here we go. Start where the framers started: with a government that had just failed. The Articles of Confederation, in force from 1781, created a league of friendship: no executive, no federal courts, no power to tax, no power to regulate commerce, and amendment only by unanimous consent. Congress could ask states for money; states could decline, and did. The design made sense to people fresh from fighting a king; it stopped making sense in 1786, when Shays' Rebellion closed courthouses in Massachusetts and the government could not raise an army to respond. Every major power in the Constitution is a patch on a named failure. Can't tax? Article one, section eight: Congress lays taxes. Can't regulate trade? The Commerce Clause. Thirteen state currencies? Only Congress coins money. No executive to enforce anything? Article two. Unanimity to amend? Now three-quarters. Read the Constitution as a bug-fix release and every clause has a reason. The Philadelphia Convention, summer 1787, was supposed to revise the Articles and instead replaced them. Three deals matter. The Great Compromise, also called the Connecticut Compromise: Virginia's plan wanted representation by population, New Jersey's plan wanted one state one vote; the deal gives you both, a House by population and a Senate with two per state. The Three-Fifths Compromise: enslaved people count as three-fifths of a person for representation and taxation, inflating Southern power in the House and the Electoral College for seventy years. And the commerce-and-slave-trade deal: Congress gets broad commerce power, but cannot touch the international slave trade until 1808, the year it promptly did. Ratification was a fight, and the fight produced the owner's manual. Federalists — Hamilton, Madison, Jay — wrote eighty-five essays as Publius: the Federalist Papers. Two get tested by name. Federalist ten, Madison: a large republic tames faction, because no single faction can dominate an extended sphere. Federalist fifty-one, Madison again: ambition must be made to counteract ambition; separation of powers works not because officials are virtuous but because their interests collide. The Anti-Federalists' price for ratification was a Bill of Rights, delivered in 1791 as the first ten amendments. Midpoint check. Why does the Constitution separate powers at all? Answer in Madison's terms. Because men are not angels: each branch is given the means and the motive to resist the others, so tyranny requires capturing all three at once. Ambition counteracting ambition. Now the machine itself. Article one, Congress, the longest article, because the framers expected it to dominate: taxing, spending, borrowing, commerce, declaring war, raising armies, naturalization, and the elastic clause — necessary and proper — which McCulloch versus Maryland read broadly in 1819. Article two, the presidency: commander in chief, treaties with two-thirds of the Senate, appointments with Senate advice and consent, the veto, and the duty to take care that the laws be faithfully executed. Article three, the courts: one Supreme Court, inferior courts as Congress creates them, judges for life during good behavior. Notice what article three does not say: judicial review. Marbury claimed it in 1803, and the system accepted the claim. The checks run in every direction, and the test loves the exact vote thresholds. Veto override: two-thirds of both houses. Treaty ratification: two-thirds of the Senate. Impeachment: a majority of the House to impeach, two-thirds of the Senate to convict. The House elects the president if the Electoral College deadlocks, each state delegation casting one vote. Judicial appointments: president nominates, Senate confirms by majority. Federalism is the vertical separation: enumerated powers to the nation, reserved powers to the states under the Tenth Amendment, concurrent powers like taxing shared by both. The Supremacy Clause of article six breaks ties: valid federal law preempts state law. The struggle over where the line sits is the whole plot of American constitutional history — remember the thread from episode one: Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, nullification, secession, and in the other direction McCulloch, Wickard, and the civil rights era's commerce power. Question three: amendment. Article five gives two proposal routes — two-thirds of both houses of Congress, or a convention called by two-thirds of the states, never yet used — and two ratification routes: three-quarters of state legislatures, or three-quarters of state conventions, used once, for the Twenty-first, repealing Prohibition, because Congress suspected the legislatures were dry. Proposal by supermajority, ratification by super-supermajority: that double gate is why ten thousand proposed amendments have produced twenty-seven survivors, and why the count includes one, the Twenty-seventh, that waited two hundred three years from proposal to ratification, 1789 to 1992, congressional pay raises deferred until an election. Recall block. What failure of the Articles did Shays' Rebellion expose? No federal power to raise an army or enforce law; the Constitution answered with a real executive and federal supremacy. The Great Compromise traded what for what? A House by population for a Senate of equal states. Federalist ten and fifty-one, one line each? Ten: a large republic tames faction. Fifty-one: ambition counters ambition. The thresholds: veto override, treaty, impeachment conviction? Two-thirds of both houses; two-thirds of the Senate; two-thirds of the Senate. The two amendment routes to proposal? Two-thirds of both houses, or a convention demanded by two-thirds of the states — the convention never yet used. Next episode: the machine in motion — how a bill really becomes law, what presidents can do alone, and the budget. Thanks for listening.
