Transcript

Episode forty-nine: the world as of September 2026 — the year's events, wired into the frames you've built. Two cautions. First, this episode is dated by design: it's current as of mid-September 2026; skim reputable coverage in the final week before your test. Second, where episodes nineteen and twenty described early 2026, this one updates them — the board has moved. Start with the hemisphere, because the year began there. On January third, American forces raided Caracas and captured Nicolás Maduro, along with his wife, flying him to New York for arraignment on narco-trafficking charges — a sitting head of state seized by military operation, something without modern precedent among even hostile powers. Wire it to your frames: the Monroe tradition and the Roosevelt Corollary revived in their most muscular form — remember the Latin America episode's ledger of interventions and the reflexes they bred; the region's reactions split along exactly those lines. The open files: Venezuela's transition — who governs, whether the eight million refugees begin returning, what happens to the oil — and the precedent question every chancery is debating: what does sovereignty mean when regime removal is a raid away? File it beside 2003's lesson: removal is the easy chapter; the state that follows is the book. The Middle East: the year's dominant war. On February twenty-eighth, Israeli and American airstrikes opened a war with Iran itself — and the opening strikes killed Ali Khamenei, Iran's Supreme Leader since 1989. Hezbollah joined within days, reopening the Lebanon front; the Houthis resumed missile fire in March — Bab el-Mandeb again. The diplomacy has been a rhythm of ceasefires and relapses: Pakistan brokered the Islamabad Memorandum in April — a US-Iran ceasefire that held three months and expired in July with fighting resumed; a separate US-brokered Israel-Lebanon ceasefire arrived in April; and as of September, hostilities continue, including an American blockade around Iranian shipping. Hold three frames from the course. The 1979 frame: this is the revolutionary republic's greatest crisis since its founding — succession after Khamenei, under bombardment, is the regime-continuity question. The chokepoint frame: Hormuz — a fifth of the world's oil — is now inside an active war zone; you know why every barrel and every insurance rate cares. And the escalation frame from 1914: alliance webs, proxies, and blockades are exactly the machinery that widens wars; the test may ask you to reason about it, not just recall it. Midpoint check. The Islamabad Memorandum was brokered by whom, between whom? Pakistan — a US-Iran ceasefire, April to July 2026. Ukraine: the war grinds toward negotiation without arriving. The year brought short truces in April and May, a Ukrainian counteroffensive, and in early September a several-day halt while American envoys — Witkoff and Kushner — shuttled between Kyiv and Moscow. No breakthrough: Russia still demands Donetsk withdrawals; Ukraine refuses unilateral concessions and wants security guarantees first; trilateral talks in the Emirates are penciled for October. Zelensky's own line — we expect the war will continue — is the honest summary. The frames hold: the episode-nineteen structure — NATO enlarged, Europe rearmed, Russia leaning on China — is unchanged; what's new is that the diplomacy is real enough to have a schedule, and the territory-versus-guarantees trade is the whole negotiation. Europe's politics turned over. Hungary first: in April, Péter Magyar's Tisza party defeated Viktor Orbán in a landslide — two-thirds of parliament, ending sixteen years of the EU's flagship illiberal democracy; the government formed in May. Wire it to the rule-of-law fights from the Europe episode: the union's most durable internal antagonist fell to a ballot box, not to Brussels — evidence in the old debate about whether pressure or patience erodes strongmen. Britain second: Keir Starmer resigned in July; Andy Burnham won the Labour leadership and became prime minister on July twentieth — the seventh premier in the decade since the Brexit vote, a churn statistic your test could quote. The continuity point beneath both: no major European government has wavered on Ukraine. The rest of the board, briskly. Asia: Myanmar's junta gained the upper hand in its civil war, with China's weight behind it — the regional-hegemony frame from the East Asia episode, applied; the Taiwan Strait produced tension without rupture — strategic ambiguity holds. Climate as event, not forecast: an August glacier collapse sent flash floods through Nepal and Tibet, killing hundreds — the Himalayan water-tower risks from the geography episode arriving on schedule. The lighter note the test won't ask but the small talk will: the first forty-eight-team World Cup ran across the United States, Mexico, and Canada — geopolitics as backdrop, from visa diplomacy to the Iran war's shadow over the draw — and Spain beat Argentina in the July final. And ahead, two dates: American midterm elections in November — the war powers, sanctions, and aid files all run through the Congress those elections choose, remember episode eight — and your test in October, which is why this episode stops here. Recall block, wiring events to frames. The Caracas raid tests which old doctrine's limits? Monroe and the Corollary — intervention's return, with 2003's state-building lesson pending. Khamenei's death poses what structural question? Succession — regime continuity in the revolutionary republic, under war. The Ukraine negotiation's core trade? Territory versus security guarantees. Hungary's April vote proved what? The flagship illiberal regime fell electorally — ballots, not Brussels. Before test day, you should? Refresh this episode against the last week's news — the board moves. That's the world as the course leaves it. Everything before this episode was built to make this one legible: doctrines, chokepoints, institutions, escalation ladders — current affairs is just history arriving. Good luck on October fourteenth. Thanks for listening.
