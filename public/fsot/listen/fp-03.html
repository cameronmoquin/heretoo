Episode nineteen: great power competition — the board as it stands. One caution up front: this episode describes the world as of early 2026; headlines will move, but the structures underneath move slowly, and structures are what the test rewards. Review first; out loud. Denying the antecedent is? Not A, therefore not B — invalid. Suez 1956 proved? The European empires now needed American permission. The Soviet Union dissolved when? Christmas 1991. Three questions. One: what replaced the unipolar moment, and what are its two rival theories of order? Two: what exactly does China contest, and with which instruments? Three: what did Russia's war in Ukraine change about European security? Here we go. Question one. From 1991 to roughly 2008, the United States stood alone — the unipolar moment. Three dates close it: 2008, Russia invades Georgia and the financial crisis dents the American model; 2012, Xi Jinping takes power with a doctrine of national rejuvenation; 2014, Russia seizes Crimea. Washington's official vocabulary since has been strategic competition — the return of great power rivalry as the organizing problem, displacing terrorism. The two theories of order in collision: the American-led rules-based international order — alliances, institutions, freedom of navigation, universal rights — against a spheres-of-influence model in which great powers command deference in their neighborhoods and sovereignty means non-interference, Westphalia quoted back at the West. Political scientists add the caution called the Thucydides trap — the structural danger when a rising power meets an established one; war is the historical tendency, not the rule, and managing the exception is the job you're testing into. Question two: China. The claims: Taiwan, treated as a domestic question with force never renounced; the South China Sea, where the nine-dash line was rejected by a 2016 Hague tribunal that Beijing ignores, while artificial islands became military bases; the Senkaku Islands dispute with Japan; and a militarized Himalayan line with India. The instruments are the interesting part, because most sit below the threshold of war. Economic: the Belt and Road Initiative, infrastructure lending across Asia, Africa, and Latin America, buying influence and ports — with debt leverage as the recurring criticism. Institutional: the Asian Infrastructure Investment Bank, BRICS expansion, the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation — parallel plumbing to Bretton Woods. Military: the largest navy by hull count, missile forces built to keep American carriers far from Taiwan — the strategy called anti-access, area denial. Gray zone: maritime militias, coast guard pressure, cyber operations — coercion calibrated to stay under the response line. And the technology front, where the competition is sharpest: American export controls since 2022 aim to hold the advanced semiconductor frontier, while both sides race in artificial intelligence; the vocabulary shifted from decoupling to de-risking — narrow the choke points, keep the trade. Taiwan sits at the center of both maps, strategic and economic, because it manufactures the overwhelming share of the world's most advanced chips — deterrence there is partly about silicon. America's declared posture on the strait remains strategic ambiguity under the Taiwan Relations Act of 1979: arm Taiwan, commit to nothing explicitly, deter both unilateral moves. Midpoint check. Why does strategic ambiguity deter both sides? Beijing can't be sure America would stay out, so invasion is risky; Taipei can't be sure America would come, so provocation is risky. Certainty in either direction frees someone to act. Question three: Russia. The road: NATO enlargement eastward through the nineties and two thousands; Putin's 2007 Munich speech declaring the unipolar order unacceptable; Georgia 2008; Crimea seized and the Donbas ignited in 2014, answered with sanctions and the halfway Minsk agreements; and February 2022, the full-scale invasion of Ukraine — the largest war in Europe since 1945. What it changed, structurally, and this is the answer worth memorizing as a list: NATO gained members — Finland joined in 2023, Sweden in 2024, doubling the alliance's border with Russia, the precise opposite of the war's stated purpose. Germany reversed decades of policy — rearmament and the end of Russian energy dependence, the Zeitenwende. Europe rebuilt its energy map away from Russian gas. Sanctions reached unprecedented scale — central bank reserves frozen, technology embargoed — pushing Russia toward China as junior partner, plus arms relationships with Iran and North Korea. Ukraine itself became the West's arsenal-supported eastern rampart, its NATO membership promised in principle and unresolved in practice — the war's central open question, along with the frozen-versus-settled shape of any end. The nuclear shadow returned to great power politics: arms control lapsed — New START, the last treaty capping American and Russian arsenals, expires in February 2026 with no successor in place — while China builds toward a much larger arsenal, making the old bilateral arithmetic three-cornered. The alliance lattice, the American answer on the board, worth knowing by name. In Europe: NATO, thirty-two members, Article five — an attack on one is an attack on all, invoked exactly once, after September eleventh, for America. In Asia, no NATO — instead a hub and spokes of bilateral treaties: Japan, South Korea, the Philippines, Australia, Thailand. The newer lattice ties the spokes together: the Quad — the United States, Japan, India, Australia, a dialogue not an alliance; AUKUS, 2021 — nuclear-powered submarines for Australia with Britain; and trilateral American-Japanese-Korean coordination. The pattern the test wants: Europe has one big treaty; Asia has many small ones, now being laced together. Recall block. The unipolar moment ran roughly? 1991 to 2008. Anti-access, area denial aims to? Keep American forces far from Taiwan — missiles against carriers. Finland and Sweden joined NATO because? Russia's 2022 invasion — the war recruited for the alliance it meant to block. The Quad's four? US, Japan, India, Australia. Article five has been invoked once — for whom? The United States, after nine-eleven. Next: the regions and flashpoints — the Middle East, Iran and the Gulf, the Koreas, and the frozen conflicts. Thanks for listening.
