Deep dive six: Cold War America at home, 1945 to 1989 — the boom, the fear, the movement, and the unraveling. Warm-up; out loud. The Depression's four gears? Banks, demand, gold, trade. The GI Bill built? The suburban middle class — unequally, through segregated channels. Withholding began? 1943. Three questions. One: what built the postwar boom, and who was fenced out of it? Two: how did the civil rights movement actually work — the machinery behind the famous moments? Three: what broke the New Deal coalition and opened the Reagan era? Here we go. Question one: the boom's ingredients. Pent-up savings from rationed war years met pent-up demand; the GI Bill pumped education and homeownership; the baby boom — seventy-six million births, 1946 to 64 — created its own economy; Bretton Woods and Europe's ruin left American industry without competitors; cheap oil powered it; and big labor's postwar bargain — the Treaty of Detroit, wages tied to productivity — spread the gains: for one generation, incomes grew fastest at the bottom. The suburbs gave it a shape: Levittown's assembly-line houses, the Interstate Highway Act of 1956 — Eisenhower's defense-justified ribbon of concrete — two cars, television, and the shopping center. Fenced out: Black families, by redlining — the government's own maps grading their neighborhoods unlendable — restrictive covenants, and Levittown's whites-only sales; the wealth gap of today compounds from exactly this era's home equity. Women, told to trade Rosie's rivet gun for the kitchen — the discontent Betty Friedan bottled as the feminine mystique, 1963. And the era's fear ran alongside its comfort: the Red Scare — Truman's loyalty program, the Hollywood blacklist, Alger Hiss, the Rosenbergs executed, and McCarthy's four-year carnival of accusation until Army counsel Joseph Welch's have you no sense of decency and a Senate censure ended him; the test wants McCarthyism as noun — accusation without evidence — and the Sputnik panic of 1957, which begat NASA and the National Defense Education Act: math and science funded as weapons. Question two: the movement's machinery — organizations, strategy, and law, behind the newsreel moments. The legal track you know through Brown; add that Brown Two's all deliberate speed invited the Massive Resistance it got — a hundred Southern congressmen signing the Southern Manifesto, Virginia closing schools rather than integrate. The organizational lattice: the NAACP's lawyers; the Southern Christian Leadership Conference — King's churches; SNCC — the students of the 1960 Greensboro sit-ins, lunch counters and voter registration; CORE riding interstate buses into firebombs in 1961 to force enforcement of desegregation rulings. The strategy was provocation by discipline: nonviolence — Gandhi via Thoreau, remember the lineage — staged where segregation would perform its own brutality for the cameras. Birmingham, 1963: Bull Connor's dogs and hoses on schoolchildren, filmed; King's Letter from Birmingham Jail answering white moderates — why we can't wait; the March on Washington that August — Randolph's old idea realized, a quarter million, I have a dream. Then the legislative harvest you know — the 64 and 65 Acts — with Freedom Summer between: a thousand volunteers, three murdered in Mississippi, the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party refused at the convention, and Selma's Bloody Sunday on the Edmund Pettus Bridge producing the Voting Rights Act within months. Results: Black registration in Mississippi from six percent to sixty in four years. And the fracture after: Watts burned days after the Voting Rights Act — de facto segregation, poverty, and police conduct in the North untouched by Southern victories; Malcolm X's by any means necessary and, after his pilgrimage, a broader vision, assassinated 1965; Black Power, the Panthers, King pivoting to Chicago housing, the Poor People's Campaign, and Vietnam — assassinated April 4, 1968, Memphis, a sanitation strike. The Kerner Commission's verdict that year: two societies, separate and unequal. Midpoint check. Why did television matter to the strategy? Discipline met brutality on camera — Birmingham and Selma made segregation perform itself before the nation and the world, forcing federal hands. Question three: the unraveling and realignment. The Great Society was the New Deal's second act — Medicare and Medicaid, 1965; the War on Poverty's Head Start and food stamps; immigration reform, 1965, ending the 1924 quotas and reopening America to Asia, Africa, and Latin America — today's demography, that law's child. But four acids ate the coalition. Vietnam split labor and students, hawks and doves — the draft's inequities, remember the conscription chain, radicalizing campuses; Tet breaking Johnson. Race: civil rights laws cost the Democrats the white South — Johnson's own prediction — and Nixon's Southern strategy collected it; busing carried the fight north, Boston's buses stoned. Crime and disorder: sixties assassinations — JFK, Malcolm, King, RFK — riots, and rising crime made law and order a ballot issue. And the seventies economy: the 1973 OPEC embargo — gas lines, oil at quadruple price — ending cheap energy; stagflation defying the Keynesian dashboard; Rust Belt deindustrialization as Germany and Japan competed again; a misery index Carter couldn't talk down, and his malaise speech read as surrender. Add Watergate's cynicism — trust in government falling from three-quarters to a quarter — plus the tax revolt spreading from California's Proposition 13, and the conservative movement built since Goldwater's 1964 rout — think tanks, direct mail, the religious right mobilized after Roe — stood ready. Reagan, 1980: government is not the solution to our problem; government is the problem. The Reagan era's ledger: inflation broken — by Volcker's brutal interest rates, begun under Carter; taxes cut, defense doubled, deficits tripled; the air traffic controllers fired, organized labor's long slide steepening; and a rhetorical restoration — morning in America — that reset politics' center of gravity for a generation, even as inequality began its long reopening. The Cold War's foreign end you know; at home, the New Deal order ended first. Recall block. The boom's bargain and its fences? Wages tied to productivity for one generation; redlining and covenants fenced Black families out of the equity. The movement's method in one sentence? Disciplined nonviolence staged to make segregation brutalize itself on camera. The 1965 immigration act did? Ended the 1924 quotas — today's demography is its child. The four acids on the coalition? Vietnam, race and busing, disorder, and the seventies economy. Who actually broke inflation? Volcker's Fed — rates near twenty percent, recession accepted. Next: the regional deep dives begin — East Asia first. Thanks for listening.
