Transcript

OMST episode four: reading comprehension. Review first; out loud. The two-clause shape of most situational judgment answers? Act within your authority, and inform the right person. The five wrong-answer archetypes? Bulldozer, ostrich, escalator, people-pleaser, deflector. Three questions. One: why is test reading a different activity from reading, and what does that change? Two: what is the six-step method? Three: what are the four trap shapes that account for most wrong answers? Here we go. Question one. Ordinary reading builds an understanding and carries it away. Test reading does something narrower: it matches a claim in an answer choice against evidence in a short passage, under a clock. That difference has a practical consequence you should take seriously, because it is the single most common cause of lost points for well-read adults. Your own knowledge is a liability in this section. If a passage discusses embassy records management and you have opinions about records management, you will find yourself choosing the option that is true rather than the option the passage supports. The test is not asking what is true. It is asking what this passage said. Read like a lawyer looking for a citation, not like a person learning something. Question two: the method, six steps. One: read the question stem first, before the passage. You want to know what you are hunting for while you read, not after. Do not read the answer choices yet; they are designed to be plausible and they will bias your reading of the passage. Stem first, options last. Two: read the passage for structure rather than detail. What is the claim, where does it turn, how does it end. Most short test passages have exactly one hinge, signaled by a word like but, however, yet, or although, and the main idea almost always lives on the far side of that hinge. If you catch the turn, you have caught the passage. Three: say the main idea in your own words before you look at any option. This step feels skippable and it is the one that protects you. An unprimed sentence of your own is the standard you then measure four engineered options against. Skip it and you are choosing between four things that all sound reasonable, with nothing to compare them to. Four: go back to the text for the specific line that settles the question. Do not answer from memory of the passage, even thirty seconds later. Detail questions and inference questions both resolve to a line or two; find them. If you cannot point at the words, you do not have the answer yet. Five: eliminate. Cross out any option that overstates, that imports a fact from outside the passage, or that answers a question you were not asked. Elimination is faster and more reliable than selection, because a wrong option usually has one identifiable defect while a right option requires you to certify everything about it. Six: choose only what the passage can support, close to word for word. The correct answer on a well-built reading item is usually a paraphrase that stays inside the passage's claims, often more modest than you expect. Midpoint check. Why read the stem before the passage but the options after? The stem tells you what to hunt for; the options are engineered to be plausible and will bias your reading if you meet them too early. Now the question types, because they repeat and each has a tell. Main idea asks for the arc of the whole passage, not its most interesting sentence; if an option captures one vivid detail, it is wrong. Detail asks what the passage literally says, and the answer is usually a near-quotation. Inference asks what must follow, and here is where your logic drills pay: the standard is must be true, not probably true, and an option that requires one extra assumption is out. Vocabulary in context asks what the word is doing in this sentence, which is frequently not its first dictionary meaning, so substitute your candidate back into the sentence and hear whether it fits. Author attitude asks for temperature, and the answer is almost always milder than the hottest options offered: explanatory, measured, cautious. Test writers offer you scathing and nostalgic; passages are rarely either. Question three: the four traps, and you can name them as fast as the situational judgment archetypes. The overstatement. Words like all, never, always, proves, entirely, and impossible. A passage that says interaction fell by roughly seventy percent does not support an option that says face-to-face conversation disappeared. Extreme language is the cheapest wrong answer to write and the easiest to spot. The outside fact. True in the world, absent from the passage. This is the trap your expertise walks you into. The test does not care that you know it; it cares whether the passage says it. The detail promoted. A real sentence from the passage, accurately reported, offered as the main idea. It is true and it is not the answer, which makes it the hardest trap to resist, because you can find it in the text. Ask: is this the arc, or one brick of it? And the reversal. The passage states a relationship in one direction; the option runs it backward. The railways imposed standard time before governments did, so an option saying governments imposed it and the railways complied is exactly wrong while sounding entirely familiar. Directional claims are worth a second read: who did what to whom, and in what order. One timing note. On a section with passages, the clock is spent in the passage, not the questions, so a passage read once properly and questions answered from targeted re-reads beats a passage read three times. And if two options survive elimination and you cannot separate them, pick the more modest one and move; the wilder claim is wrong far more often than not. Recall block. What is the standard for an inference? Must be true, not probably true. Name the four traps. Overstatement, outside fact, detail promoted to main idea, reversal. What do you do before looking at any answer choice? State the main idea in your own words. Where does the main idea usually live in a short passage? On the far side of the hinge: after the but, however, or yet. When two options survive, which do you pick? The more modest one. Next episode: the hard situational cases, where the rules override ordinary helpfulness. Thanks for listening.
