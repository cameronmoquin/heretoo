The Supreme Court by Era

Transcript

Episode ten: the Supreme Court by era. Review; out loud. If A then B, B, therefore A — name the flaw. Affirming the consequent. Where do most bills die? Committee. Youngstown's lowest ebb is presidential action against what? The express will of Congress. Three questions. One: how did a court with no army and no purse become a co-equal branch? Two: which court expanded rights fastest, and through which amendment? Three: what has the modern court been taking apart, and with what tools? The trick for the whole episode: don't memorize cases as a list; memorize each court as a character with one project. Character one: the Marshall Court, 1801 to 1835, and its project is building federal power from paper. John Marshall, a Federalist appointed in Adams' last days, runs the Court for thirty-four years. Marbury versus Madison, 1803: judicial review — the Court claims the power to strike down federal laws, cleverly, in a case where the ruling gave Jefferson nothing to defy. McCulloch versus Maryland, 1819: implied powers are real, and no state may tax the national bank — the power to tax involves the power to destroy. Gibbons versus Ogden, 1824: the commerce power belongs to Congress, broadly read. Worcester versus Georgia, 1832: Cherokee sovereignty, the ruling Jackson ignored. Marshall's project succeeds so thoroughly that every later fight happens on his field. Character two: the Taney Court, whose one unforgettable act is Dred Scott, 1857 — Black Americans are not citizens, Congress cannot ban slavery in the territories. It took the Civil War and three amendments to overrule, and it stands as the cautionary tale: the Court can wound itself for a generation. Then the long post-war retreat: the Slaughter-House Cases, 1873, gut the Fourteenth Amendment's privileges clause; the Civil Rights Cases, 1883, hold the amendment reaches only state action, not private discrimination; Plessy, 1896, blesses separate but equal; Lochner, 1905, strikes a maximum-hours law under liberty of contract, naming an era where economic regulation loses; Schenck, 1919, upholds Espionage Act convictions — clear and present danger, Holmes' phrase. The pattern: rights narrowed, business protected. Character three you already know from episode four: the New Deal collision. Schechter kills the National Recovery Administration in 1935; Roosevelt threatens the court-packing plan; West Coast Hotel, 1937, is the switch in time; Wickard, 1942, stretches commerce to wheat a farmer feeds his own animals. Add the war's shame, Korematsu, 1944, internment upheld — repudiated by the Court itself in 2018. After 1937 the Court leaves economics to Congress and turns its attention to rights. Which sets up the answer to question two. Midpoint check. What single doctrinal move made the rights revolution possible? Incorporation: reading the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause to apply the Bill of Rights against the states, piece by piece. Before incorporation the Bill of Rights bound only Washington; after, every sheriff, school board, and state legislature. So, character four: the Warren Court, 1953 to 1969, project: incorporation and equality, at speed. The roll call, and note the rhythm — one case, one right. Brown versus Board, 1954: separate is inherently unequal, Plessy overruled for schools. Mapp, 1961: illegally seized evidence excluded in state courts. Engel, 1962: no state-composed school prayer. Gideon, 1963: a lawyer for every felony defendant who cannot pay. New York Times versus Sullivan, 1964: public officials must prove actual malice to win libel suits — breathing room for a free press. Griswold, 1965: a right of privacy in the penumbras, contraception for married couples. Miranda, 1966: you have the right to remain silent, and police must say so. Loving, 1967: interracial marriage protected. Tinker, 1969: students don't shed speech rights at the schoolhouse gate. Baker versus Carr and Reynolds versus Sims: one person, one vote, forcing legislative districts to equal population. No court before or since moved rights that far that fast. The Burger Court consolidates rather than reverses: Pentagon Papers, 1971, no prior restraint; Roe, 1973, abortion within privacy; United States versus Nixon, 1974, unanimous — the tapes must be produced, executive privilege exists but yields to criminal process; Buckley versus Valeo, 1976, money as speech in campaigns; Bakke, 1978, race as one factor, quotas forbidden. Character five: the Rehnquist and Roberts Courts, and question three's answer — the project is retrenchment, pulling doctrines back. Federalism: Lopez, 1995, the first commerce-power limit since 1937 — guns near schools are not commerce. Elections: Bush versus Gore, 2000, ends a recount and a presidency's suspense; Citizens United, 2010, independent corporate political spending is protected speech; Shelby County, 2013, disables the Voting Rights Act's preclearance formula; Rucho, 2019, partisan gerrymandering declared beyond federal courts' reach. Rights recast: Heller, 2008, an individual Second Amendment right; Obergefell, 2015, same-sex marriage — the era's major expansion; Dobbs, 2022, Roe overruled, abortion returned to the states. And the administrative state: West Virginia versus EPA, 2022, the major questions doctrine — big regulatory moves need clear congressional authorization; Loper Bright, 2024, Chevron deference overruled — courts, not agencies, now settle ambiguous statutes; SFFA, 2023, race-conscious admissions ended; Trump versus United States, 2024, substantial immunity for official presidential acts. The through-line: power returning from agencies and precedents to courts, states, and explicit statutes. Recall block. Each court's one project — Marshall? Building federal power. Warren? Incorporation and equality. Roberts? Retrenchment. Which case pairs overrule each other? Plessy and — Brown. Roe and — Dobbs. Chevron and — Loper Bright. Miranda, Gideon, Mapp — which right is which? Miranda, warnings before interrogation; Gideon, appointed counsel; Mapp, exclusion of illegal evidence. What did Marbury never actually order? Anything Jefferson could refuse — the genius was claiming the power while giving the opponent no order to defy. Next: the amendments in runs, and the liberties they carry. Thanks for listening.
