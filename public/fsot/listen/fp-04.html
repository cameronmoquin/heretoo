Episode twenty: regions and flashpoints — the rest of the board, region by region. Same caution as last time: structures as of early 2026, built to outlast headlines. Review; out loud. Strategic ambiguity deters both sides because? Neither Beijing nor Taipei can count on America's choice. Asia's alliance architecture differs from Europe's how? Hub and spokes of bilateral treaties, not one big NATO. Belt and Road buys? Infrastructure, influence, and ports — with debt as leverage. Three questions. One: what are the Middle East's three overlapping contests? Two: why is North Korea's bomb rational from Pyongyang's chair? Three: where are the frozen conflicts, and who keeps each one frozen? Here we go. The Middle East, question one, three contests stacked on one map. Contest one: Israel and the Palestinians. The lineage you carry from episode seventeen — Balfour, mandate, 1948 partition and war, then 1967's Six-Day War, when Israel took the West Bank, Gaza, Sinai, and the Golan Heights; UN Resolution 242, land for peace, has framed diplomacy since. Camp David, 1978, traded Sinai back for Egypt's recognition — Carter's triumph, the first Arab peace. Oslo in the nineties created the Palestinian Authority but the final-status questions — borders, Jerusalem, refugees, settlements — were never closed; Gaza fell to Hamas in 2007 after Israel's withdrawal, and Hamas's October 2023 attack and the devastating Gaza war that followed reopened every file, with a two-state settlement professed abroad and remote on the ground. Contest two: Iran versus the Gulf Arabs and Israel — a Shia revolutionary republic since 1979 facing Sunni monarchies, waged through proxies: Hezbollah in Lebanon, militias in Iraq and Syria, the Houthis in Yemen, who turned Red Sea shipping into a target range after 2023 — remember Bab el-Mandeb from the geography lesson. Over it hangs the nuclear file: the JCPOA of 2015 traded enrichment limits for sanctions relief; America withdrew in 2018; Iran's program has since advanced to near-weapons threshold, and the deal's ghost haunts every regional calculation, alongside the twelve-day Israel-Iran war of June 2025 that struck the program directly. Contest three: the American question — how much presence? The Abraham Accords of 2020 — Israel normalized with the UAE, Bahrain, and Morocco — rested on a bet that Arab-Israeli alignment against Iran could outflank the Palestinian issue; Saudi normalization, the great prize, waits on Gaza's aftermath. Meanwhile Syria's civil war ended its Assad chapter in December 2024 with the regime's sudden fall — a reminder, like 1989, that frozen-looking orders can liquefy overnight. Midpoint check. Resolution 242's formula, three words? Land for peace — territory returned in exchange for recognition and security. Question two: the Koreas. The armistice of 1953 never became a peace treaty; the Demilitarized Zone is the world's most fortified border, Seoul within artillery range. The North tested its first device in 2006 and now holds dozens of warheads with intercontinental missiles in development. Why rational? Pyongyang watched Iraq and Libya give up their programs and lose their regimes; the bomb is the Kim dynasty's insurance policy, which is why sanctions and summits — including the Trump-Kim meetings of 2018 and 19 — have never bought disarmament, only pauses. The realistic policy menu is deterrence and containment, not denuclearization; the North's new arms-for-troops relationship with Russia since 2024 raised its leverage further. South Korea, meanwhile, is the alliance system's showcase — a poor autocracy in 1960, a rich democracy and cultural exporter now — and the third of the trilateral lattice with Japan and America. Question three: the frozen conflicts, learned by patron, because the patron is the answer. Russia keeps: Transnistria, a Soviet remnant wedged in Moldova; South Ossetia and Abkhazia, carved from Georgia in 2008; and kept the Armenian side in Nagorno-Karabakh — until Azerbaijan retook it outright in 2023 while Russia was busy in Ukraine, proof that frozen conflicts thaw when the freezer loses power. Turkey keeps Northern Cyprus, divided since 1974, an EU member state partly occupied. Morocco holds Western Sahara. Somaliland governs itself, recognized by no one. Kashmir divides nuclear India and Pakistan along the Line of Control — three wars and counting. And the island chains from the geography lesson — Senkaku, Kurils, Spratlys, Dokdo — are maritime versions of the same genre: sovereignty asserted, resolution postponed. The remaining regions in one pass each. Africa: the world's youngest and fastest-urbanizing population; the African Union and the continental free trade area on the institutional side; the Sahel on the crisis side — coups across Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger displaced French forces, with Russian mercenaries stepping in, while civil war shattered Sudan from 2023 and Ethiopia's Tigray war closed bloodily before it. The competition dimension: Chinese infrastructure and minerals, Gulf money, and everyone's embassies multiplying — Africa is where great power competition meets demography. Latin America: no interstate wars, but state weakness — Venezuela's collapse produced the hemisphere's largest refugee outflow, nearly eight million; organized crime stresses Ecuador and Haiti; Brazil and Mexico anchor the economies; and the American policy files are migration, fentanyl, and countering Chinese investment — the Monroe Doctrine's ghost in modern dress. South Asia beyond Kashmir: India as the world's most populous state and its swing power — Quad member, Russian arms customer, nobody's ally, everyone's suitor. And the global commons, where new files open: the Arctic, melting into a navigable, contested ocean flanked by a now nearly all-NATO coastline minus Russia; cyberspace, conflict below the war threshold as a daily condition; and space, congested and militarized, from anti-satellite tests to constellation internet in wartime — Ukraine ran partly on commercial satellites, a first. Recall block. The Middle East's three contests? Israel-Palestinians; Iran versus Gulf Arabs and Israel through proxies and the nuclear file; and the scale of American presence. The Abraham Accords bet what? That Arab-Israeli alignment against Iran could outflank the Palestinian question. Pyongyang's bomb is? Regime insurance — the Iraq-Libya lesson. Who keeps Northern Cyprus, and who held Karabakh until 2023? Turkey; Russia's freezer failed and Azerbaijan took Karabakh. Venezuela's collapse produced? The hemisphere's largest refugee outflow — nearly eight million. Next: money — the economic thought chain, from mercantilism to the moderns. Thanks for listening.
