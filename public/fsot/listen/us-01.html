The Founders' Republic, 1789 to 1825

Transcript

Welcome to the FSOT audio course. This is episode one: the Founders' Republic, 1789 to 1825. Five presidents, one question asked over and over: how strong should the national government be? Hold that question in mind, because almost every fact in this era is an answer to it. The five presidents, in order: Washington, Adams, Jefferson, Madison, Monroe. The mnemonic: Will A Jolly Man Make. Before we start, three questions. You're not supposed to know the answers yet; trying anyway primes your memory for what's coming. One: what rebellion proved the new Constitution could enforce its own laws, and how? Two: which Supreme Court case established judicial review, and which one confirmed implied powers? Three: why did Jefferson, the strict constructionist, swallow his principles in 1803? Keep those open. Here we go.

Start with George Washington, 1789. Everything he does becomes a precedent because nobody has done it before. He assembles the first cabinet, and inside that cabinet sits the whole future of American politics: Alexander Hamilton at Treasury, Thomas Jefferson at State. Hamilton wants a national bank, federal assumption of state debts, tariffs, manufacturing, a loose reading of the Constitution. Jefferson wants farms, states' rights, and a strict reading. Their fight creates the first party system: Hamilton's Federalists against Jefferson's Democratic-Republicans. In 1794, farmers in western Pennsylvania refuse to pay Hamilton's whiskey tax. Washington personally leads thirteen thousand militiamen to crush the Whiskey Rebellion. Remember what that proves: unlike under the Articles of Confederation, when Shays' Rebellion went unanswered by a helpless Congress, the new federal government can enforce its own laws. If a test question pairs Shays and Whiskey, that contrast is the answer. Washington leaves in 1797 with a Farewell Address warning against permanent alliances and party spirit. That address is the first link in the foreign policy chain we'll follow all course long: stay out of Europe's wars. John Adams, president number two. One term, two things to know. First, the XYZ Affair: French agents demand bribes just to open negotiations, America is outraged, and an undeclared naval Quasi-War with France follows. Second, the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, which made it a crime to criticize the government. Jefferson and Madison answer with the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions, arguing states can judge federal laws unconstitutional. File that idea away: it resurfaces in 1832 as nullification, and again in 1861 as secession. The test loves that thread. The election of 1800 puts Jefferson in power, and here is why it matters beyond Jefferson: it is the first peaceful transfer of power between opposing parties in modern history. Adams' party loses, and simply hands over the government. Jefferson calls it the Revolution of 1800. But Adams leaves a parting gift: last-minute judicial appointments, the midnight judges. One undelivered commission produces Marbury versus Madison in 1803, where Chief Justice John Marshall establishes judicial review, the Court's power to strike down laws as unconstitutional. Same year, 1803, Jefferson doubles the country: the Louisiana Purchase, fifteen million dollars to Napoleon. Notice the irony the test likes: the strict constructionist finds nothing in the Constitution authorizing a land purchase, and does it anyway. Lewis and Clark go out to map it. Jefferson's second term sours at sea. Britain and France, at war with each other, seize American ships; Britain impresses American sailors into its navy. Jefferson's answer is the Embargo Act of 1807: stop trading with everyone. It devastates New England's economy and barely touches Europe. Economic coercion fails. Quick midpoint check; answer out loud. Why did the Embargo Act backfire? Because America needed the trade more than Europe did: it strangled New England's merchants while Britain and France barely noticed. Economic coercion without leverage punishes yourself. So James Madison, president four, inherits the problem, and in 1812 the War Hawks in Congress push him into war with Britain. The War of 1812 gives you four images: the British burn Washington and the White House; Fort McHenry survives bombardment and gives us the Star-Spangled Banner; New England Federalists meet at the Hartford Convention to flirt with secession, and destroy their party when the war ends before their complaints land; and Andrew Jackson wins the Battle of New Orleans in January 1815, two weeks after the peace treaty was signed at Ghent, because news traveled by ship. The war ends in a draw, but it feels like a win, and it launches Jackson. James Monroe, president five, presides over the Era of Good Feelings, one party, the Federalists dead. Three landmarks. First, McCulloch versus Maryland, 1819: Marshall again, ruling the national bank constitutional under implied powers, and that no state may tax it. The line to remember: the power to tax involves the power to destroy. Second, the Missouri Compromise of 1820: Missouri enters as a slave state, Maine as a free state, and a line is drawn across the Louisiana territory at thirty-six degrees thirty minutes. North of the line, no slavery. That line is the fuse of the Civil War, and it's where our next episode begins. Third, the Monroe Doctrine of 1823: Europe stays out of the Americas, the second link in the foreign policy chain. Let's close the loop. The era's question was: how strong should the national government be? The answers stacked up on the strong side: the whiskey tax enforced, judicial review claimed, implied powers confirmed, territory doubled by executive action. And the counter-tradition, the Virginia and Kentucky idea that states can refuse, is loaded and waiting. Quick recall before we go. Say each answer out loud in the pause. Who were the five presidents of the Founders' Republic, in order? Washington, Adams, Jefferson, Madison, Monroe. Will A Jolly Man Make. Which 1803 case established judicial review? Marbury versus Madison. What did the Missouri Compromise draw, and where? A line at thirty-six thirty across the Louisiana Purchase; slavery banned north of it. Which two documents form the first two links of the foreign policy chain? Washington's Farewell Address, and the Monroe Doctrine. Next episode: the staircase to the Civil War, where every compromise undoes the last. Thanks for listening.
