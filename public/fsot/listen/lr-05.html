Logic Bootcamp, Assumptions

Logical Reasoning · 6:29 · Episode 41

Open the full player

Transcript

Logic bootcamp one: assumptions, the whole hour on the single most valuable question type. Warm-up from the regions; out loud. The two September elevenths? New York 2001; Santiago 1973. Bolívar plowed? The sea — institutions missing, caudillos filling. Now the drill. The method, recited once more, because today we stress-test it: an assumption is the unstated premise the argument cannot live without; find it by negation — deny the candidate, and if the argument dies, that was it. The distractors are always the same three impostors: something the argument doesn't need, something too strong, or something that merely restates the conclusion. Argument twice, question, silence, answer aloud. One. The new consular software processes applications faster than clerks do. Adopting it will therefore shorten visa wait times. Again: the software processes faster than clerks; adopting it will shorten waits. The assumption? That processing speed is the bottleneck. Negate: suppose waits are driven by interviews or background checks — then faster processing shortens nothing, and the argument dies. Note the pattern, because it recurs in every efficiency argument: improving a step helps only if that step is the constraint. Two. Ambassador Reyes speaks daily with the foreign minister. She must therefore be well informed about the government's intentions. Again: daily conversations with the minister; therefore well informed about intentions. Assumption? That the minister's words track the government's actual intentions — that he knows and tells. Negate: if the minister is out of the loop, or lying, daily access informs her of nothing. Access is not accuracy; sources must be both placed and truthful. Three. Countries with professional civil services suffer less corruption. Therefore merit-based hiring reduces corruption. Again: professional services correlate with less corruption; therefore merit hiring reduces it. The assumption? The causal direction — that professionalism produces cleanliness rather than clean countries affording professionalism, and that no third factor — wealth, rule of law — drives both. You know this beast: it's the correlation-to-cause leap, and stated as an assumption it becomes: no reverse causation, no common cause. Same animal, assumption costume. Four. This year's summit will succeed, because both presidents want an agreement. Again: both presidents want agreement, so the summit will succeed. Assumption? That wanting suffices — that no obstacle beyond the principals' desire can block it: legislatures, spoilers, deal-breaking details. Negate: if wanting isn't enough, desire proves nothing. Watch for arguments that treat intentions as outcomes; diplomacy's graveyard is paved with mutual desire. Five. The ministry denies persecuting journalists, and no journalist has produced documents proving otherwise. So the accusations are baseless. Again: denial plus no documentary proof; therefore baseless. The assumption? That persecution would have produced accessible documents — that absence of proof here means absence of fact. It's the appeal to ignorance wearing an assumption mask, and the negation kills it: persecution designed to leave no paper leaves no paper. Six — now distinguish necessary from sufficient assumptions, the refinement the hardest questions test. The argument: officers who complete area studies perform better at post; therefore the department should fund area studies for all officers. Two candidate assumptions. A: area studies is the single most effective training investment. B: the performance gains stem at least partly from the training itself. Which is necessary? B. The argument needs the training to cause some gain — negate B and it collapses. A is stronger than needed: funding can be justified without area studies being the best investment. The trap: test-writers offer the grander statement hoping you'll grab it; the necessary assumption is the humblest claim that keeps the argument breathing. When two candidates both help, take the weaker one. Seven, the full anatomy, you alone. Because the rebel faction signed the ceasefire, and ceasefires reduce violence, the province will be safer next year. Again: the faction signed; ceasefires reduce violence; so the province will be safer. Name two assumptions, then negate each. First: this faction will honor its signature — negate, they signed to rearm, and safety evaporates. Second: this faction's violence is the operative threat — negate, rival factions or bandits dominate, and their ceasefire changes little. Signatures bind only signers, and only sincere ones. The bootcamp's residue, five reflexes; answer each. The bottleneck reflex? A fix helps only if it fixes the constraint. The source reflex? Placed and truthful — access is not accuracy. The direction reflex? No reverse causation, no common cause. The desire reflex? Intentions are not outcomes. The strength reflex? Between two helpful candidates, the necessary one is the weaker. Next bootcamp: inference — what must be true, and the discipline of refusing to know more than the passage says. Thanks for listening.
