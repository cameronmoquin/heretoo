The Doctrine Chain, Washington to Now

World Affairs · 7:44 · Episode 13

Open the full player

Transcript

Episode thirteen: the doctrine chain — American foreign policy from Washington's Farewell to today, told as one continuous argument. Review first; out loud. The weakener for any member-versus-nonmember comparison? Selection effect. The two First Amendment religion clauses? Establishment and free exercise. Sixteen-seventeen-eighteen-nineteen are whose amendments? The Progressives'. Three questions. One: what single tension runs through every American doctrine from 1796 to now? Two: which doctrine answers which — who is replying to whom? Three: which institutions were built to carry the policy, and in which years? Here's the frame for question one right away: every doctrine is a new answer to one question — how entangled should America be with the world? The pendulum swings out and back, and each swing has a name. Swing one: stay out. Washington's Farewell Address, 1796 — no permanent alliances; commerce with all, entanglement with none. Jefferson adds no entangling alliances, and the young republic holds that line for a century, with one addition: the Monroe Doctrine, 1823 — Europe stays out of the Americas. Note what Monroe is not: it isn't engagement with the world; it's a fence around the hemisphere, drafted with British naval power quietly behind it, by Secretary of State John Quincy Adams — the test likes his authorship. Swing two: the hemisphere becomes a beat to patrol. The Roosevelt Corollary, 1904, inverts Monroe: not only must Europe stay out — the United States itself will police the Americas, collecting debts and landing Marines. Speak softly, carry a big stick. Taft follows with dollar diplomacy, influence through loans and investment; Wilson with moral diplomacy. Then the Great War drags America in, and Wilsonianism, 1918, is the first swing to full engagement: the Fourteen Points, self-determination, open covenants, a League of Nations. You know from episode four how that ends — Lodge, article ten, the Senate's no — and the pendulum snaps back: the twenties and thirties are isolation, high tariffs, the Neutrality Acts, while FDR's Good Neighbor policy, 1933, at least retires the Marines from Latin America. Midpoint check. The Roosevelt Corollary modified which doctrine, and how? Monroe's. Monroe said Europe keep out; the Corollary added: and we will intervene ourselves to keep order — the fence became a beat. Swing three: permanent engagement — the Cold War architecture, and the answer to question three arrives with it. Truman Doctrine, 1947: support free peoples resisting subjugation — containment operationalized, Greece and Turkey first. The same burst builds the machinery: the Marshall Plan for Europe's economies; the National Security Act, 1947, creating the CIA, NSC, and the unified Defense Department; NATO, 1949, the permanent alliance Washington's Farewell warned against — the pendulum's full arc in one hundred fifty-three years. The earlier institutional layer matters too: the Rogers Act, 1924, created the professional Foreign Service itself — your exam's own ancestry — merging the diplomatic and consular corps into one merit-based career service. Then the Cold War sequence you can already recite: Eisenhower Doctrine, 1957, resisting communism in the Middle East; Kennedy's flexible response, retiring massive retaliation; the Nixon Doctrine, 1969, allies carry their own defense burden — Vietnamization — plus détente and the China opening; the Carter Doctrine, 1980, the Persian Gulf is a vital American interest, answered to the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan; the Reagan Doctrine, support anti-communist insurgents — Afghanistan, Nicaragua — rollback replacing containment's patience. Alongside: USAID born 1961, the Peace Corps the same year, War Powers 1973, Goldwater-Nichols 1986 reorganizing the military into joint commands, and after September eleventh, the Director of National Intelligence, 2004, coordinating the agencies that had failed to share. Swing four: after the wall, each presidency writes its own answer. Bush forty-one: the new world order — coalition warfare under UN blessing, the Gulf War as proof of concept. Clinton: enlargement — expand the community of market democracies; NATO grows eastward. Bush forty-three, 2002: preemption — after nine-eleven, America will strike threats before they form; Afghanistan, then Iraq. Obama: retrenchment and rebalance — the pivot to Asia, the Iran nuclear deal, the opening to Cuba. Trump: America First — tariffs, alliance skepticism, and the Abraham Accords normalizing Israel's relations with Gulf states. Biden: alliance restoration — NATO reinvigorated and enlarged, Ukraine armed, the Indo-Pacific latticework of AUKUS and the Quad. Different names; same old question — how entangled? — now asked about China instead of Europe. The reply chain, question two, in fast pairs, answer the second half out loud. Farewell Address says no alliances; which doctrine finally reverses it? NATO, 1949 — permanent alliance. Monroe fences the hemisphere; who turns the fence into a beat? The Roosevelt Corollary. Wilson's League dies in the Senate; what succeeds where it failed? The UN, 1945 — this time with America inside, and a veto. Massive retaliation is answered by? Flexible response. Containment's patience is answered by? The Reagan Doctrine's rollback. Recall block. The one question every doctrine answers? How entangled should America be with the world. The 1947 burst — name the doctrine, the plan, and the act. Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, National Security Act — CIA, NSC, DoD. Rogers Act, 1924, did what? Created the professional, merit-based Foreign Service. Carter Doctrine's one sentence? The Persian Gulf is a vital American interest — force authorized to defend it. Preemption belongs to whom, and answering what? Bush forty-three, 2002, answering nine-eleven. Next: the machinery of diplomacy itself — State, the UN system, Bretton Woods, treaties versus executive agreements, and the tools between talking and shooting. Thanks for listening.
