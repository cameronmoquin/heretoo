Transcript

Episode twenty-three: trade, currencies, and the trilemma — economics where diplomacy lives. Review; out loud. The Fed's workhorse? The federal funds rate. GDP counts production where — domestic or by ownership? Domestic — within the borders; GNP tracks ownership. A price ceiling below equilibrium creates? Shortage. Three questions. One: if trade enriches both sides, why does it stay politically radioactive? Two: what makes a currency rise or fall, and who wins each way? Three: what is the impossible trinity, and which two corners has each major economy picked? Here we go. Ricardo told you trade pays in aggregate. Question one is about the word aggregate: the gains are broad and thin — every consumer saves a little — while the losses are narrow and thick: a steel town loses everything at once. Concentrated losers organize; diffuse winners don't — that asymmetry is the whole politics of trade, and it's also why economists pair trade liberalization with adjustment assistance in theory and why the assistance underwhelms in practice. The protection toolkit: tariffs — taxes on imports, the Constitution's original revenue source; quotas — quantity limits, worse than tariffs because the government doesn't even collect the revenue; subsidies to home industries; non-tariff barriers — standards and paperwork as disguised walls; and dumping — selling abroad below cost to kill competitors, the accusation that launches a thousand WTO cases. The classic cautionary tale you already hold: Smoot-Hawley, 1930, retaliation spiraling world trade downward into the Depression. The institutions from episode fourteen referee it: GATT rounds cut tariffs for fifty years, the WTO since 1995 binds disputes — and its appellate body has been paralyzed since 2019 by American refusal to seat judges, so the referee currently has no whistle. Regional deals fill the gap: NAFTA, renegotiated as USMCA in 2020; the EU, the deepest integration — single market, one currency for most; the CPTPP, the Pacific pact America negotiated, abandoned in 2017, and watched others join. Question two: currencies. An exchange rate is just a price — dollars per euro — set, for floating currencies, by supply and demand: demand for a country's exports, its interest rates pulling in capital, and confidence. Higher American interest rates strengthen the dollar: capital flows in chasing yield. A strong dollar: imports cheap, foreign travel cheap — but American exports pricey abroad, manufacturing squeezed, and, crucially for the Foreign Service, emerging-market debts denominated in dollars get heavier. A weak dollar mirrors it: exporters smile, consumers pay. So who wins is always the question's real subject — devaluation is export promotion wearing a currency costume, which is why competitive devaluation gets policed and why currency manipulator is a formal American designation. The dollar itself is the system's anchor: the world's reserve currency — the majority of global reserves, trade invoicing, and the plumbing of international banking — which grants America the exorbitant privilege of borrowing cheaply in its own money, and the sanctions leverage you met in episode fourteen: exclusion from dollar clearing is exile from world finance. Rivals — the euro, the yuan, gold, crypto — chip at the edges; none yet threatens the core, though sanctions overuse is the standard argument for why they might. Midpoint check. Higher American interest rates do what to emerging markets? Pull capital out and strengthen the dollar — their currencies fall, their dollar debts get heavier; the Fed's medicine is their fever. Question three: the trilemma, the impossible trinity — a country may have at most two of: a fixed exchange rate, free capital flows, and independent monetary policy. Why: with capital free and the rate pegged, your interest rates must follow the anchor currency's, or money floods in or out until the peg breaks — your monetary policy is spent defending the peg. You met this in the logic drills as Ruritania; here's the real map. The United States, Japan, the United Kingdom: free capital plus independent policy — the currency floats. The Eurozone members: free capital plus a fixed rate with each other — one currency — so no member has its own monetary policy; the European Central Bank sets one rate for all, which is exactly why Greece in 2010 couldn't devalue its way out and endured austerity instead: the trilemma written in Greek. China, historically: a managed rate plus independent policy — by restricting capital flows, the corner most economies gave up. And Bretton Woods itself was a trilemma settlement: fixed rates plus policy autonomy, purchased with capital controls; when capital mobility returned, the system cracked — Nixon closed the gold window in 1971, and the world floated. Balance of payments, the bookkeeping behind it: the current account — trade in goods and services plus income flows — mirrors the capital account; a trade deficit means foreigners accumulate your assets. America's persistent deficit is thus also a persistent sale of IOUs the world happily holds — because of the reserve currency, the circle closes. The IMF from episode fourteen is the emergency room when pegs and payments fail: dollars lent, conditions attached — austerity, reform — and resentment of conditionality is a standing theme of the Global South's politics. Recall block. Why is trade politically hard despite aggregate gains? Losses concentrate, gains diffuse — the losers organize. A strong dollar hurts whom at home and abroad? American exporters; emerging markets with dollar debts. The trilemma's three corners? Fixed exchange rate, free capital, independent monetary policy — pick two. Greece 2010 illustrated? No national monetary policy inside the euro — austerity instead of devaluation. Nixon in 1971 did what to money? Closed the gold window — Bretton Woods pegs ended, currencies floated.
