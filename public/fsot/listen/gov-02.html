Episode eight: Congress, the presidency, and the money. Review; out loud. The two routes to proposing an amendment? Two-thirds of both houses, or a convention called by two-thirds of the states. Which Federalist paper says ambition must counteract ambition? Fifty-one, Madison. What clause did McCulloch read broadly? Necessary and proper — the elastic clause, implied powers. Three questions. One: where do most bills actually die, and why is that the design working, not failing? Two: what can a president do entirely alone, and where does that power stop? Three: who writes the budget, and what happens when they don't? Here we go. The two chambers are built to different clocks. The House: four hundred thirty-five members, two-year terms, apportioned by census every ten years, majoritarian to its bones — the Rules Committee decides what reaches the floor and under what terms, and a determined majority can do nearly anything. The Speaker is second in the line of succession, after the vice president. The Senate: one hundred members, six-year terms staggered in thirds, and a set of rules that protect the minority — above all the filibuster: most legislation now needs sixty votes to close debate, called invoking cloture. The carve-outs matter: budget reconciliation moves fiscal legislation by simple majority, and confirmations, both executive and judicial, now need only a simple majority after the rules changes of 2013 and 2017. Unique Senate powers: treaties and confirmations. Unique House powers: originating revenue bills and initiating impeachment. How a bill becomes law, the real version. Introduction, then committee — and question one answers itself here: committee is where most bills die, by simple neglect, and that is the design. Committees are the filter that lets thirty thousand introduced bills compete for a floor calendar with room for hundreds. Survivors get a markup, a report, then the floor: House under a rule, Senate under unanimous consent or cloture. Both chambers must pass identical text — differences go to a conference committee or bounce between chambers. Then the president: sign; veto, returnable by two-thirds of both houses; do nothing for ten days, and it becomes law without signature; or, if Congress has adjourned inside those ten days, the pocket veto, which cannot be overridden. There is no line-item veto — Congress tried to create one by statute, and Clinton versus City of New York struck it down in 1998: the president signs or vetoes bills whole. Midpoint check. Why does the pocket veto exist, and when does it work? It works only when Congress adjourns within the ten-day window, leaving the president no chamber to return the bill to. Ordinary silence enacts; adjournment-silence kills. Question two: the president alone. The unilateral toolkit: executive orders, which direct the executive branch and carry the force of law only where the Constitution or a statute grants the underlying power; executive agreements, international commitments made without Senate ratification, valid but only as durable as the next administration; pardons and reprieves for federal offenses, nearly unlimited, but not for impeachments and not for state crimes; recognition of foreign governments; and command of the military — but declaring war belongs to Congress, and after Korea and Vietnam the War Powers Resolution of 1973 requires notice within forty-eight hours and withdrawal after sixty days without authorization. The boundary case every test loves: Youngstown, 1952. Truman seized the steel mills during the Korean War; the Court said no, and Justice Jackson's concurrence gave the framework — presidential power is at its maximum with Congress behind it, in a twilight zone when Congress is silent, and at its lowest ebb against Congress's express will. Executive power is real, revocable, and bounded by statute: that's the whole doctrine in one sentence. Impeachment, since it keeps happening. The House impeaches by simple majority — that's an indictment, not a removal. The Senate tries the case, two-thirds to convict, the Chief Justice presiding when it's the president. Three presidents impeached — Andrew Johnson, Clinton, and Trump twice — zero convictions. Nixon resigned before the House voted. The penalty is removal, and optionally disqualification from future office; criminal courts remain separate. Question three: the money. The Constitution gives Congress the purse; the modern process comes from the Budget Act of 1974, the same era as War Powers — Congress clawing power back. The president proposes a budget in February; Congress passes a budget resolution, then twelve appropriations bills fund the government by October first, the fiscal new year. When they don't pass — and they usually don't — Congress buys time with continuing resolutions, and if even those lapse, a shutdown: non-essential operations stop. Separate and regularly confused: the debt ceiling, which is not about new spending at all, but about borrowing to pay for spending already enacted. Two more definitions the test likes: mandatory spending — Social Security, Medicare, Medicaid, interest — runs on autopilot and is roughly two-thirds of the budget; discretionary spending, defense and everything else, is the third Congress actually votes on each year. And the Government Accountability Office and Congressional Budget Office are Congress's own auditors and scorekeepers — nonpartisan, and frequently the referee in fiscal fights. Recall block. Where do most bills die? In committee, by design — the filter that makes a floor calendar possible. Cloture takes how many votes, and what are the two big exceptions? Sixty; reconciliation and confirmations move by simple majority. Youngstown's three zones? Maximum power with Congress, twilight when Congress is silent, lowest ebb against Congress's will. The debt ceiling authorizes what? Borrowing for spending already enacted — not new spending. Pocket veto conditions? Congress adjourns within the ten-day window; no return possible, no override.
