Regional deep dive four: Africa — fifty-four states, three inheritances, and the century being decided there now. Warm-up; out loud. The Schuman logic? Pool war industries; make war impossible. Russia's pattern? Buffer, modernize from above, harden, collapse, repeat. Dayton froze? Bosnia's war into a constitution. Three questions. One: what did colonialism actually leave behind — the three inheritances? Two: which five states anchor the continent's five regions, and what is each one's file? Three: what changed in the last decade — the new scramble, and Africa's own hand? Here we go. Question one, the inheritances. First: the borders — Berlin's lines from episode sixteen, drawn by rulers who had mostly never seen the land, splitting some nations across two or three states and binding rivals inside one. The independence generation, knowing redrawing meant endless war, froze them: the Organization of African Unity's 1964 Cairo rule made colonial borders sacrosanct — so the map's absurdities became permanent, and the wars moved inside the lines: secessions failed in Biafra and Katanga; the exceptions that made it, Eritrea in 1993 — landlocking Ethiopia, remember — and South Sudan in 2011, promptly consumed by its own civil war. Second: the state design — colonies were built for extraction, not governance: railways run from mine to port, not city to city; administration thin, and indirect rule empowering chosen intermediaries — Belgium's favoritism between Hutu and Tutsi in Rwanda hardened categories that became the 1994 genocide's fault line: eight hundred thousand dead in a hundred days while the UN withdrew — the failure that haunts every later intervention debate. Third: the economies — monoculture and minerals: cocoa in Ghana, copper in Zambia, oil in the Delta — the commodity trap: booms without diversification, busts without cushions, and the resource curse where oil or diamonds fund the fight for the state instead of the state itself — Angola, Sierra Leone, the DRC's coltan wars. The independence arc after: liberation heroes becoming presidents-for-life — Nkrumah's Ghana first free in 1957, then one-party states and Cold War proxy patronage; the 1990s bringing multipartyism with the Cold War's end; and the scorecard today genuinely mixed — Botswana and Mauritius as governance stars, Ghana and Senegal as democratic anchors, and the coup belt relapsing. Question two, the five anchors. West: Nigeria — the giant: most populous, biggest economy, oil-rich and oil-cursed, split north-south between Muslim and Christian halves, fighting Boko Haram in the northeast; if Nigeria succeeds, West Africa succeeds — the embassy cliché that happens to be true. East: Ethiopia — never colonized, remember Adwa; the African Union's host, a developmental state on the Asian model until the Tigray war broke its momentum; the dam on the Nile and the lost coastline are its standing files. South: South Africa — apartheid's fall in 1994, Mandela's miracle of truth-and-reconciliation instead of civil war; the continent's most industrial economy, hobbled now by state capture's corruption legacy and power cuts; the diplomatic voice of the Global South, BRICS member. Center: the Democratic Republic of the Congo — the reverse anchor: Leopold's genocidal rubber colony, Mobutu's Cold War kleptocracy — the West's man, remember Lumumba's murder — then Africa's world war after the Rwandan genocide spilled west: five million dead by 2003, the deadliest conflict since 1945, and the east still burning over minerals your phone contains. North: Egypt — the Arab world's demographic center, the Suez state, army-ruled — you know it from the Spring; add the Nile arithmetic binding it to Ethiopia's dam. Midpoint check. Why did the OAU freeze Berlin's borders? Redrawing by nation would have meant wars without end — sanctifying the arbitrary map was the least-war option. Question three: the new scramble and the African hand. The suitors: China first — the largest trading partner and infrastructure builder, Belt and Road's biggest theater, minerals-for-ports-and-rails, plus its first overseas base in Djibouti — remember why Djibouti; the debt-trap accusation and the built-actual-things rebuttal both live on your test. Russia sells security — Wagner's successors trading gold and mining rights for regime protection across the Sahel, filling France's vacated space. The Gulf states buy ports and farmland along the Red Sea corridor. The West rebrands: aid recast as investment partnerships, minerals security — cobalt, lithium — driving new attention; Europe's migration anxieties funding border projects. The African hand strengthening: the African Continental Free Trade Area from 2021 — the world's largest free-trade zone by membership, integrating fifty-four markets against the colonial rail-to-port design; the African Union joining the G20; and demographic leverage — remember the geography episode: the youngest, fastest-growing population on earth, a quarter of humanity by century's end, Lagos and Kinshasa among the world's largest cities. The two futures on one continent, and the test's balanced sentence: dividend if education and jobs arrive — the dragons' recipe, African edition; danger where they don't — the Sahel's coups, jihadist insurgencies from Mali to Lake Chad, and climate stress on the margins feeding both. Hold both; questions punish single-story answers. Recall block. The three inheritances? Frozen arbitrary borders; extraction-built states; commodity-trap economies. Rwanda 1994's numbers and lesson? Eight hundred thousand in a hundred days; the intervention failure that haunts the doctrine. The five anchors? Nigeria, Ethiopia, South Africa, the DRC, Egypt. Africa's world war centered where, costing what? The Congo — five million dead by 2003. The free trade area fights which inheritance? The extraction map — integrating African markets with each other at last. Next region: Latin America — the hemisphere the Monroe Doctrine claimed, and what it makes of that claim now. Thanks for listening.
