OMST, The Hard Cases

Transcript

OMST episode five: the hard cases. Review; out loud. The four reading traps? Overstatement, outside fact, detail promoted to main idea, reversal. The standard for an inference? Must be true. The scenarios in this episode are the ones candidates get wrong, and they share a structure: an ordinary virtue points one way and a rule points the other. Loyalty to a colleague, helpfulness to a friend, deference to a superior, discretion about an awkward situation. Each is a genuine good, and each is the wrong answer in the right circumstances. Twelve cases, and each one wants two answers, because the section asks for the best action and the worst action out of the same set of options. Give the worst first. A clearly wrong action is easier to recognise than a best action competing with a good one, and naming it shortens the field. The four questions do the separating. Does it break a rule. Does it act on the problem, or work around it. Does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it. Is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Nothing here asks you to have read Department procedure. Scene, silence, both picks aloud. One. Your supervisor instructs you to do something you believe conflicts with policy. Worst pick, then best. Worst: comply without saying anything, on the theory that the instruction was theirs and so is the accountability. That works around the problem instead of acting on it. Best: ask your supervisor to clarify the instruction and raise your specific concern; if it is not resolved, go to the office that owns the policy. Misunderstandings are far more common than misconduct, so the conversation comes first and the escalation path stays open behind it. Reporting your supervisor before you have spoken to them fails two questions at once: wrong person, disproportionate to what you know. Two. A supervisor asks you to record hours on a timesheet you did not work, calling it a routine correction. Worst, then best. Worst: enter the hours. It breaks a rule, and the friendly label does not change what it is, which is falsification of a federal record. Best: decline, and if it is repeated, report it through the appropriate channel. Compare case one. There the act was ambiguous, so the clarifying conversation was owed; here it is clearly improper, so it is not. Three. You notice a pattern in expense documentation suggesting funds may be misused, but you are not certain. Worst, then best. Worst: ask a few colleagues whether they have noticed it too. That reaches nobody who can fix it and turns a concern into a rumor. Best: report the observation through the appropriate channel, management or the Inspector General. Certainty is the investigator's job, and waiting until you are sure means waiting while it continues. Confronting the employee yourself is the other trap: disproportionate to what you know, and it can compromise an investigation. Four. You made an error that nobody has discovered yet, and it may cause a problem next week. Worst, then best. Worst: wait and see whether it actually causes the problem. Best: fix what you can, then report the error and its likely effects to your supervisor promptly. An error discovered later is also a concealment discovered later, and the second one is what ends careers. Five. At a reception, a pleasant acquaintance asks which countries the Ambassador will visit next month. Worst, then best. Worst: confirm part of it, the dates or one country, because part feels like discretion. It is a disclosure, and pattern information is exactly what is being fished for. Best: say only that the Ambassador travels frequently, and do not discuss the schedule. And declining because the trip is classified is itself a disclosure. The friendly non-answer is the skill. Six. A locally employed colleague you like and rely on asks whether you could help a relative get an earlier visa appointment. Worst, then best. Worst: get the appointment moved, or pass the name to someone who can. Insider access applied to one case is improper however small the push. Best: decline warmly but clearly, and explain that appointments come only through the published process. The tempting middle option is the vague yes you then quietly fail to deliver, which works around the problem and costs the relationship twice. Seven. A senior officer asks you to use the motor pool for a personal errand during work hours. Worst, then best. Worst: run the errand, because he is senior. Rank does not convert a personal errand into official business. Best: politely decline, noting that the vehicle is for official use. Offering to do it on your own time does not fix it either, because the resource is the vehicle, not your hours. Eight. A visitor you do not know arrives without an appointment, well dressed, insisting the matter is urgent and that the Ambassador will want to see them. Worst, then best. Worst: give them the front office's direct number and let them arrange it themselves. That hands an unvetted caller a principal's line and leaves no record of the visit. Best: courteously take their name, affiliation, and purpose, and check with the front office and security before any access is granted. Access is a security function wearing the costume of a courtesy question. Nine. You find an unlabeled USB drive on the floor inside the building. Worst, then best. Worst: plug it into a stand-alone machine to identify the owner. It sounds cautious and it is still a compromise. Best: turn it in to the information systems security officer without connecting it to anything. Found media is a standard attack vector, and helpfulness is not worth the risk. Ten. A visiting American officer speaks dismissively to a locally employed colleague in front of you. Worst, then best. Worst: say nothing and hope the visit ends soon. Silence tells your colleague the treatment is acceptable. Best: show the colleague visible professional courtesy immediately, and raise the behavior privately with the visitor or their control officer. Correcting the visitor in front of the room creates a second incident. Model the standard in the moment, address the behavior in private. Eleven. In a meeting, a colleague presents work you prepared as their own. Worst, then best. Worst: email the section your timestamped draft. It is disproportionate to what you know, and it goes to everyone instead of the one person who could put it right. Best: speak with them privately afterward, describe what happened, and ask how they intend to correct it. Correcting the record publicly in the moment escalates before you know whether it was theft or thoughtlessness. Private first; the fact that you have the draft does not expire. Twelve. You cannot meet a deadline, and you know it midway through. Worst, then best. Worst: hand in whatever is finished at the deadline without comment. It spends someone else's time discovering what you already knew. Best: tell your supervisor as soon as you know, with a revised estimate and what could be dropped or shifted to meet the original. When the deliverable is at risk, the warning becomes the deliverable. Working late and hoping is the same failure in a more flattering costume. Step back and take the pattern, because it generalizes past these twelve. In every hard case the wrong answers are generated by real virtues. The test knows that, and builds the tempting option out of whichever virtue fits the scene. So when an option feels good and something in you resists it, run the four questions on the option rather than on your conscience. One caution. This episode oversamples rule cases, because rules are where the virtues bite hardest. Across forty keyed items the option that breaks a rule was the rarest worst answer, and the option that works around the problem instead of acting was the commonest. In the ordinary run of items, the thing that does nothing is what you hunt first. Recall block. Which pick do you make first, and why? The worst; a clearly wrong action is easier to recognise than a best action competing with a good one. Ambiguous instruction from a supervisor versus a clearly improper one: what differs? Ambiguous gets a clarifying conversation first; clearly improper gets a plain refusal. Why is saying a trip is classified the wrong non-answer? It discloses information about the trip. When the deliverable is at risk, what is the deliverable? The early warning. What generates every tempting wrong answer in the hard cases? A real virtue, fitted to the scene. Next episode: the last one, the final week and the test day itself. Thanks for listening.
