OMST, The Hard Cases

Transcript

OMST episode five: the hard cases. Review; out loud. The four reading traps? Overstatement, outside fact, detail promoted to main idea, reversal. The standard for an inference? Must be true. The scenarios in this episode are the ones candidates get wrong, and they share a structure: an ordinary virtue points one way and a rule points the other. Loyalty to a colleague, helpfulness to a friend, deference to a superior, discretion about an awkward situation. Each is a genuine good, and each is the wrong answer in the right circumstances. Twelve cases. Same format: scene, silence, answer aloud. One. Your supervisor instructs you to do something you believe conflicts with Department policy. What do you do? Ask your supervisor to clarify the instruction and raise your specific concern; if it is not resolved, seek guidance from the office that owns the policy. Note what this is not. It is not silent compliance on the theory that the supervisor is accountable, and it is not refusing and reporting them. Misunderstandings are far more common than misconduct, so the conversation comes first, and the escalation path stays open behind it. Two. A supervisor asks you to record hours on a timesheet you did not work, calling it a routine correction. What do you do? Decline, and if it is repeated, report it through the appropriate channel. Compare that with case one and notice the difference: this is not an ambiguous instruction, it is falsification of a federal record, and the friendly label does not change what it is. The line between the two cases is whether the act itself is clearly improper. If it is, you do not need the clarifying conversation. Three. You notice a pattern in expense documentation suggesting funds may be misused, but you are not certain. What do you do? Report the observation through the appropriate channel, such as management or the Inspector General. Certainty is the investigator's job, not yours; waiting until you are sure means waiting while it continues. And do not confront the employee, and do not canvass colleagues to see whether they noticed too, because both can compromise an investigation and both are how a concern becomes a rumor. Four. You made an error that nobody has discovered yet, and it may cause a problem next week. What do you do? Fix what you can, then report the error and its likely effects to your supervisor promptly. This is the self-report rule and it is worth stating plainly: an error discovered later is also a concealment discovered later, and the second one is what ends careers. Reporting it yourself, early, with an assessment of the damage, is the answer every time. Five. At a reception, a pleasant acquaintance asks which countries the Ambassador will visit next month. What do you do? Say only that the Ambassador travels frequently, and do not discuss the schedule. Two subtleties. Confirming any part of it, even just the dates, gives away pattern information. And saying you cannot discuss it because the trip is classified is itself a disclosure about the trip. The friendly non-answer is the skill. Six. A locally employed colleague you like and rely on asks whether you could help a relative get an earlier visa appointment. What do you do? Decline warmly but clearly, and explain that appointments come only through the published process. The difficulty here is not knowing the rule, it is paying the social cost of applying it to someone you work with every day and genuinely like. Warmth and clarity together: the relationship survives a clear no far better than it survives a vague yes that you then quietly fail to deliver. Seven. A senior officer asks you to use the motor pool for a personal errand during work hours. What do you do? Politely decline, noting that the vehicle is for official use. Rank does not convert a personal errand into official business, and doing it on your own time does not fix it either, because the resource is the vehicle, not your hours. Eight. A visitor you do not know arrives without an appointment, well dressed, insisting the matter is urgent and that the Ambassador will want to see them. What do you do? Courteously take their name, affiliation, and purpose, and check with the front office and security before any access is granted. Access is a security function wearing the costume of a courtesy question. And never hand out a principal's direct number to an unvetted caller, which is the option the test offers as the helpful compromise. Nine. You find an unlabeled USB drive on the floor inside the building. What do you do? Turn it in to the information systems security officer without connecting it to anything. Found media is a standard attack vector. The tempting wrong answer is plugging it into a stand-alone machine to identify the owner, which sounds cautious and is still a compromise; helpfulness is not worth the risk. Ten. A visiting American officer speaks dismissively to a locally employed colleague in front of you. What do you do? Show the colleague visible professional courtesy immediately, and raise the behavior privately with the visitor or their control officer. Correcting the visitor publicly creates a second incident, and saying nothing at all tells your colleague the treatment is acceptable. Model the standard in the moment, address the behavior in private. Eleven. In a meeting, a colleague presents work you prepared as their own. What do you do? Speak with them privately afterward, describe what happened, and ask how they intend to correct it. Correcting the record publicly in the moment escalates before you know whether it was theft or thoughtlessness, and emailing the section your timestamped draft is the same escalation with evidence attached. Private first; the fact that you have the draft does not expire. Twelve. You cannot meet a deadline, and you know it midway through. What do you do? Tell your supervisor as soon as you know, with a revised estimate and what could be dropped or shifted to meet the original. When the deliverable is at risk, the warning becomes the deliverable. Working late and hoping is the ostrich; delivering something partial at the deadline without comment is worse, because it spends someone else's time discovering what you already knew. Step back and take the pattern, because it generalizes past these twelve. In every hard case the wrong answers are generated by real virtues: loyalty, helpfulness, deference, discretion, diligence. The test knows that, and builds the tempting option out of whichever virtue fits the scene. So when an option feels good and something in you resists it, name the category first. If it is security, ethics, or safety, the rule wins and the virtue is the trap. If it is ordinary judgment, then the virtues are back in play and the method decides. Recall block. Ambiguous instruction from a supervisor versus a clearly improper one: what differs? Ambiguous gets a clarifying conversation first; clearly improper gets a plain refusal. Why not confront the employee in a suspected funds case? It can compromise an investigation, and certainty is not your job. Why is saying a trip is classified the wrong non-answer? It discloses information about the trip. When the deliverable is at risk, what is the deliverable? The early warning. What generates every tempting wrong answer in the hard cases? A real virtue: loyalty, helpfulness, deference, discretion, diligence. Next episode: the last one, the final week and the test day itself. Thanks for listening.
