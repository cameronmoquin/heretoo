Episode twenty-seven: statistics — enough to read any chart, poll, or claim the test throws at you. Review, at speed. Rule of seventy at five percent? Fourteen years to double. Down twenty then up twenty lands at? Ninety-six. Weighted averages lean toward? The bigger group. Three questions. One: when do mean and median disagree, and which should you trust? Two: what does a margin of error actually promise, and what shrinks it? Three: which statistical sins reappear on every test — and in every newspaper? Here we go. Question one: the centers. The mean is the total divided by the count; the median is the middle value; the mode is the most common. They agree on symmetric data and split when the data is skewed — and income is the eternal example. Nine people earn fifty thousand and one earns a million. Median? Fifty thousand. Mean? One hundred forty-five thousand — the millionaire drags it. So: means chase outliers; medians resist them. When a question says average income rose, ask which average — a boom at the top raises the mean and leaves the median untouched, and the gap between them is itself a measure of inequality. Test reflex: skewed data, trust the median; the word typical usually means median; a mean far above the median means a long right tail. Spread completes the picture: the range is max minus min, fragile to one outlier; the standard deviation measures typical distance from the mean. And for the normal distribution — the bell curve — the rule of thumb: about sixty-eight percent of values within one standard deviation, ninety-five within two. An IQ-style test with mean one hundred and deviation fifteen puts ninety-five percent of people between? Seventy and one thirty. Percentiles read the same curve: the ninetieth percentile beats ninety percent of the field — and note, a score at the fiftieth percentile is the median by definition. Question two: sampling. A poll of a thousand people speaks for millions only if the sample is random — everyone equally likely to be asked. The margin of error, for that thousand, is about plus or minus three points at ninety-five percent confidence — meaning: if the poll ran many times, ninety-five of a hundred runs would bracket the truth; it is not a guarantee about this one run. The scaling law is the useful part: the margin shrinks with the square root of the sample. To halve the margin? Quadruple the sample — four thousand people for plus or minus one and a half. A quick mental formula: margin is roughly one over the square root of N. Four hundred people gives? About five percent. Twenty-five hundred gives? About two percent. And the killer fact: population size barely matters — a good thousand-person sample serves a city or a continent equally well; what matters is randomness. Which is where polls actually fail: non-random samples. Selection bias — polling landlines, or mall shoppers; non-response bias — the angry answer, the content hang up; survivorship bias — studying the businesses that lived, the planes that returned. The classic: armorers reinforced returning bombers where they saw holes, until someone noted the holes marked survivable hits — armor where the returners were clean. When a sample selects itself, no sample size saves it — remember the logic drills: selection effect, same beast, statistical costume. Midpoint check. A two-point lead with a three-point margin means? A statistical tie — the margin swallows the lead; either candidate may be ahead. Question three: the sins, as a spotter's guide. Sin one: correlation dressed as causation — you own this one from the logic drills; the statistical version adds the confounder, the third variable moving both: ice cream and drownings rise together because summer moves both. Sin two: the missing base rate. A test that's ninety-nine percent accurate for a condition one in ten thousand people have will still be wrong most of the times it says yes — the false positives from the healthy multitude swamp the true positives from the rare few. Screening questions punish anyone who ignores how rare the thing is. Sin three: truncated axes and cherry-picked windows — a chart starting at ninety-eight makes a two-point difference look like a cliff; a trend measured from an unusual year — remember your baseline drills — proves whatever the chooser wants. Ask: where does the axis start, and why does the series start there? Sin four: Simpson's paradox, the aristocrat of traps — a trend that holds in every subgroup can reverse in the total. A university can admit men at a higher overall rate while every department admits women at a higher rate, if women applied to the tougher departments. When aggregated and disaggregated numbers disagree, the grouping variable is doing the work; ask what's being pooled. Sin five: the regression fallacy — extreme performances drift back toward average on the retest, coaching or no coaching; the sophomore slump and the miracle turnaround are often just regression to the mean. And sin six, your old friend: rates versus counts — more shark attacks in America than Malta says beaches, not danger; always ask per what? Recall block. Skewed data — which center survives? The median; the mean chases the tail. Ninety-five percent of a normal curve sits within? Two standard deviations. Halving the margin of error costs? Four times the sample. The bomber armor story teaches? Survivorship bias — study what didn't come back. Simpson's paradox is? Subgroup trends reversing in the aggregate — ask what's pooled. Ice cream and drowning share? A confounder — summer. Next: English expression — the grammar the test actually tests. Thanks for listening.
