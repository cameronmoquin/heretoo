Episode three: the Civil War, Reconstruction, and the Gilded Age, 1861 to 1900. First, review. Answer out loud in the pauses. What did the Kansas-Nebraska Act repeal? The Missouri Compromise line; popular sovereignty replaced it, and the Republican party was born in protest. What did Dred Scott rule about Congress and the territories? That Congress had no power to ban slavery in any territory. What happened in April 1861 at Charleston harbor? Confederate guns fired on Fort Sumter, and the war began. Three questions for this episode. One: why did Lincoln wait for a battlefield victory before announcing emancipation? Two: what deal ended Reconstruction, and what replaced it in the South? Three: why did the world's first regulatory agency appear in 1887, of all years? Here we go. The war's logic is simple: the North has everything except good generals; the South has generals and nothing else. Twenty-two million people against nine million, of whom three and a half million are enslaved. Ninety percent of the industry is Northern. So the North's plan, the Anaconda, is to squeeze: blockade the coast, take the Mississippi, split the Confederacy. The South's plan is to make the war so expensive the North quits. That means the South doesn't need to win; it needs to not lose. Every battle you memorize fits that frame. The early war goes South. Bull Run in 1861 shatters the illusion of a short war. Then September 1862, Antietam in Maryland: the single bloodiest day in American history, about twenty-three thousand casualties. It's tactically a draw, but Lee retreats, and that's victory enough for what Lincoln has been waiting to do. Five days later he announces the Emancipation Proclamation, effective January 1, 1863. Now, why did he wait for a win? Because announced from weakness it would read as desperation, a losing side inciting slave revolt. Announced from strength, it transforms the war's meaning: now every Union advance frees people, and Britain and France, whose textile mills crave Confederate cotton but whose publics hate slavery, can never recognize the South. One document, and Europe is checkmated out of the war. Note the proclamation's limit, a favorite test trap: it frees slaves only in areas in rebellion, not the loyal border states. Full abolition waits for the Thirteenth Amendment. July 1863, the double blow. Gettysburg turns back Lee's invasion of the North in Pennsylvania; the same week, Grant takes Vicksburg, and the Mississippi is Union top to bottom. The Confederacy is cut in two. From here it's arithmetic: Grant grinds Lee in Virginia while Sherman takes Atlanta, then marches to the sea, burning the South's will and rail lines both. April 1865: Lee surrenders at Appomattox. Five days later, Lincoln is shot dead by John Wilkes Booth. So Reconstruction begins under the worst possible president for it: Andrew Johnson, a Tennessee Democrat kept on the ticket for unity, hostile to Black rights, generous to ex-Confederates. Congress fights him with the Reconstruction amendments; learn them as one, two, three. Thirteen: slavery abolished, 1865. Fourteen: citizenship and equal protection, 1868. Fifteen: the vote regardless of race, 1870. Johnson obstructs so relentlessly that the House impeaches him in 1868; the Senate falls one vote short of removal. Midpoint check. Why does the Fourteenth Amendment matter so far beyond 1868? Because equal protection becomes the hook for almost every rights case after: Brown, Loving, Obergefell. When a modern rights question appears, the Fourteenth is usually the answer. Under Grant, Reconstruction is enforced at gunpoint: federal troops, the Klan Acts. But the North tires. The contested election of 1876 produces the Compromise of 1877: Hayes gets the White House; in exchange, the troops leave the South. Reconstruction is over. What replaces it? Jim Crow: segregation laws, poll taxes, literacy tests, and the Supreme Court's blessing in Plessy versus Ferguson, 1896, separate but equal. The Fifteenth Amendment stays on paper; the vote disappears in practice for nearly a century. Now flip the coin, because while the South is being abandoned, the North is being transformed. This is the Gilded Age, Mark Twain's name, gold on the surface, iron underneath. Railroads stitch the continent, the golden spike at Promontory in 1869. Carnegie in steel, Rockefeller in oil, Morgan in finance; call them captains of industry or robber barons, both names are the test's way of asking the same question. Immigration surges through Ellis Island; cities explode; political machines like Tammany Hall trade services for votes. And the losers of this economy organize. Farmers, crushed by railroad shipping rates set by monopolies, form the Grange, then the Populist Party, demanding free silver, inflation to shrink their debts. Labor organizes too, and gets broken repeatedly: Haymarket 1886, Homestead 1892, Pullman 1894, federal troops against strikers. Which answers our third question: why 1887? Because that farmer fury finally forced Congress to act on the railroads. The Interstate Commerce Act of 1887 created the Interstate Commerce Commission, the first federal regulatory agency, and the Sherman Antitrust Act followed in 1890 under Benjamin Harrison. Remember the trap from the lessons: Theodore Roosevelt used the Sherman Act later; he did not write it. When a question says first regulatory agency, the answer is the ICC, 1887, railroads. The era closes with America stepping onto the world stage. 1898: the battleship Maine explodes in Havana harbor, the yellow press howls, and the Spanish-American War lasts ten weeks. Spain hands over Cuba's independence, plus Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. America is suddenly a colonial power arguing with itself about whether it should be one. McKinley wins re-election on prosperity in 1900, and is shot in 1901, which hands the presidency to a forty-two-year-old Theodore Roosevelt. That's next episode. Recall block; out loud. Why did Lincoln time emancipation to Antietam? He needed to announce it from strength; it redefined the war and locked Europe out. Thirteen, fourteen, fifteen: what does each do? Abolition; citizenship and equal protection; the vote regardless of race. What did the Compromise of 1877 trade? Hayes takes the presidency; federal troops leave the South; Jim Crow follows, blessed by Plessy in 1896. First federal regulatory agency, year, and target? The Interstate Commerce Commission, 1887, railroads. Next episode: the Progressives repair capitalism, a war to end all wars doesn't, and the Crash brings the state into everything. Thanks for listening.
