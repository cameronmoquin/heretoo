Reform, Boom, and Bust, 1900 to 1945

Transcript

Episode four: reform, boom, and bust, 1900 to 1945. Review first; out loud in the pauses. The three Reconstruction amendments, in one breath? Thirteen abolition, fourteen citizenship and equal protection, fifteen the vote regardless of race. What was the first federal regulatory agency? The Interstate Commerce Commission, 1887, aimed at railroads. What did America take from Spain in 1898? Cuba freed, and Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines taken. This episode answers three questions. One: what four amendments passed in a single seven-year burst, and why then? Two: why did the Senate reject Wilson's League of Nations? Three: what changed constitutionally in 1937 that made the New Deal survivable? Here we go. The Progressive Era's premise: industrial capitalism produced miracles and horrors, and government should fix the horrors without killing the miracle. The muckrakers supply the horror stories: Ida Tarbell on Standard Oil, Upton Sinclair's The Jungle on meatpacking, so nauseating it produced the Meat Inspection Act and the Pure Food and Drug Act within months, 1906. Theodore Roosevelt is the era's engine. His Square Deal: control the corporations, protect consumers, conserve nature. He dusts off the Sherman Act against the Northern Securities railroad trust in 1904 and wins; the trust-buster is born. He mediates the 1902 coal strike instead of shooting at strikers, the first president to treat labor as a party rather than a problem. He sets aside national forests and parks. Abroad, he takes Panama from Colombia by supporting its secession, builds the canal, and adds the Roosevelt Corollary to the Monroe Doctrine: the United States will police the Americas itself. Speak softly and carry a big stick. Roosevelt hands off to Taft, who actually busts more trusts, including Standard Oil in 1911, but manages to alienate Roosevelt anyway. In 1912 Roosevelt comes back to run against his own protégé on the Bull Moose ticket, splits the Republican vote, and hands the presidency to the Democrat, Woodrow Wilson. That's the pattern to remember: third parties in America don't win; they decide who does. Wilson's first term is the legislative jackpot: the Federal Reserve Act of 1913, finally giving the country a central bank after Jackson killed the last one; the Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914, which strengthens Sherman and exempts labor unions; the Federal Trade Commission, same year. And the amendments: sixteen, income tax, 1913. Seventeen, direct election of senators, 1913. Eighteen, Prohibition, 1919. Nineteen, women's suffrage, 1920. Four amendments, seven years. Why then? Because Progressivism was a movement about trusting the people over the interests: taxing wealth directly, taking senators from the state legislatures the machines controlled, banning the saloon, and doubling the electorate. Same impulse, four applications. Sixteen through nineteen equals Progressive; that clustering answers test questions by itself. Then the war. America stays out of Europe's 1914 catastrophe until German submarines and the Zimmermann Telegram, Germany inviting Mexico to attack the United States, drag Wilson in, April 1917. America's weight decides it by November 1918. Wilson arrives at Versailles with his Fourteen Points, self-determination and a League of Nations, and comes home with the League written into the treaty. And the Senate says no. Why? Article ten. Senators led by Henry Cabot Lodge argued the League's collective-security clause could commit American troops to war without Congress declaring it. Wilson refused compromise, stumped the country, broke his health, and the treaty failed. The United States never joins the League, and the retreat into isolation begins. Midpoint check. The 1920s in one sentence: what's the policy mood? Government steps back: Harding's normalcy and the Teapot Dome bribery scandal, Coolidge declaring the business of America is business, tax cuts, tariffs up, immigration slashed by the quota acts of 1921 and 1924. The market roars on margin debt. October 1929, the Crash, and then the misery compounds: banks fail by the thousands, a third of the money supply evaporates, unemployment hits twenty-five percent. Hoover, whose name becomes a shantytown, believes in voluntary cooperation and balanced budgets; the Smoot-Hawley Tariff of 1930 strangles world trade and exports the Depression. In 1932, Franklin Roosevelt wins forty-two states promising a New Deal. The New Deal comes in two waves. The first hundred days, 1933: bank holiday and the FDIC to stop the runs, Glass-Steagall separating banking from speculation, the SEC for Wall Street, the CCC and WPA for jobs, the TVA for power, the AAA for farms. The second wave, 1935: Social Security, and the Wagner Act guaranteeing the right to unionize. Relief, recovery, reform, the three R's. But the Supreme Court starts striking it down, Schechter kills the NRA in 1935. Roosevelt, re-elected in a landslide, threatens to pack the Court with six new justices. The plan fails in Congress, it's his worst political defeat, but in 1937 Justice Roberts starts voting to uphold, West Coast Hotel, the switch in time that saved nine. That's the answer to our third question: after 1937 the Court reads the commerce power broadly, Wickard versus Filburn in 1942 stretches it to a farmer's own wheat, and the constitutional wall against federal economic regulation is gone for sixty years. Then the world catches fire again. America's instinct is neutrality, the Neutrality Acts, but Roosevelt bends it step by step: cash and carry, the destroyer deal, and Lend-Lease in March 1941, the arsenal of democracy lending Britain the tools. December 7, 1941, Japan bombs Pearl Harbor; Germany declares war on America four days later, solving Roosevelt's biggest political problem for him. The war at home: industry converts totally, Rosie the Riveter, and the shame of Executive Order 9066, internment of a hundred twenty thousand Japanese Americans, upheld in Korematsu, 1944. The war abroad, four hinges: Midway, June 1942, Japan's navy broken; Stalingrad, the Nazi tide turned in the East; D-Day, June 6, 1944, the Normandy landings; and the Manhattan Project's bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, August 1945, after Roosevelt has died and Harry Truman has made the call. Victory, and a new world: only two powers left standing, and the alliance between them already cracking. Recall block. The four Progressive amendments, sixteen through nineteen? Income tax, direct election of senators, Prohibition, women's suffrage. Why did the Senate kill the League? Article ten's war commitments versus Congress's war power; Lodge versus Wilson; no compromise. The switch in time: what changed in 1937? The Court stopped striking New Deal laws; the commerce power stretched, from West Coast Hotel to Wickard. Order these: Pearl Harbor, Lend-Lease, D-Day, Midway. Lend-Lease, March 1941; Pearl Harbor, December 1941; Midway, June 1942; D-Day, June 1944. Next episode: containment, the Cold War chain from the Long Telegram to the fall of the Wall, and the era we live in now. Thanks for listening.
