Transcript

Episode forty-five: development economics — why nations escape poverty, why some don't, and what the aid business learned the hard way. Warm-up; out loud. The four logic habits? Name the question; hold inferences to must; negate for assumptions; judge causes against rivals and counterfactuals. The dragons' recipe? Land reform, exports, education, state-guided credit. Effect equals? Outcome minus counterfactual. Three questions. One: what are the competing explanations for why some nations are rich — geography, culture, institutions, history? Two: what are the named traps — poverty trap, resource curse, middle-income trap — and their mechanisms? Three: what does the evidence say aid can and cannot do? Here we go. Question one, the grand debate, four schools. Geography: landlocked, tropical, malarial — remember the geography episodes — distance and disease taxing everything; the school's evidence is real, the objection stronger: Singapore is tropical, Botswana landlocked, both rich — geography loads the dice but doesn't roll them. Culture: thrift, trust, work ethic — Weber's old Protestant-ethic claim; the objection is that culture explains best in hindsight: Confucian values were cited to explain Asian stagnation before 1960 and Asian miracles after — an explanation that predicts both ways predicts neither. History as legacy: extraction's long shadow — colonial institutions built to mine, not govern, remember Africa's inheritances and Latin America's haciendas; the natural experiment the literature loves: settler colonies got settlers' institutions, extraction colonies got extraction's, and the income gap tracks it centuries later. Which folds into the current consensus, institutions: inclusive rules — property rights, contract enforcement, constraint on rulers, broad participation — versus extractive ones that concentrate gain; the Korea experiment from the regions episode is the cleanest proof, one people, two institution sets, night and day from orbit. The synthesis for the test: geography deals the hand, history shapes the institutions, institutions play the cards. Question two, the traps, each a mechanism. The poverty trap: too poor to save, too unproductive to grow — subsistence eats every surplus; escape requires a push — health, roads, seeds — that lifts productivity above the treadmill; malaria control and the Green Revolution's high-yield wheat and rice — Borlaug's Nobel — are the historic examples: India from famine watch to grain exporter inside a generation. The resource curse, from the Africa episode, now with mechanisms: Dutch disease — resource exports strengthen the currency, remember the economics of exchange rates, pricing your factories and farms out of world markets, so the oil crowds out everything else; volatility — budgets riding commodity prices, boom-borrowing then bust-austerity; and politics — a state funded by wells needs no taxpayers, and a state that doesn't need taxpayers doesn't need their consent: rents finance repression or civil war for the tap. The exceptions prove management matters: Norway's fund, Botswana's diamonds — institutions again. The middle-income trap: the dragons' recipe stalls — cheap-labor exports lose to cheaper labor elsewhere before innovation replaces them; escaping means the hard upgrade to skills and technology — Korea and Taiwan made it; much of Latin America, remember import substitution's whiplash, circled at middle income for decades. China's current struggle against exactly this trap is the biggest economic story on your test's horizon. Midpoint check. Dutch disease in one sentence? Resource exports inflate the currency until everything else the country makes is uncompetitive — the oil eats the factories. Question three: aid, honestly. The record is genuinely mixed, and the test rewards knowing both columns. What works, with strong evidence: health — smallpox eradicated 1980, the century's best purchase; PEPFAR, the American AIDS program from 2003, credited with twenty-five million lives, diplomacy's proudest line item; vaccines through Gavi, oral rehydration, bed nets — cheap, measurable, scalable; humanitarian relief and the Green Revolution's agronomy. What disappoints: budget support to weak institutions — fungible money feeding the patronage it means to bypass; grand infrastructure without maintenance — the graveyard of donated tractors; and aid as geopolitics — Cold War clients, remember Mobutu, where the point was alignment, not development. The famous debate: Sachs's big push — poverty traps need big money — versus Easterly's planners-versus-searchers — top-down schemes fail where feedback is absent — versus the randomistas — Banerjee, Duflo, Kremer, Nobel 2019 — who stopped asking does aid work and started testing which aid works, program by program, with the randomized trials your logic bootcamps just taught you to demand: treatment, control, counterfactual. Meanwhile the real flows dwarf the debate: remittances several times aid, remember; private investment larger still; and China's Belt and Road lending, which reframed the field as competition — the reason development is now a strategic cone skill, not a charity sideline. The Foreign Service framing to carry: development is stability policy — the Marshall Plan's original insight, desperate economies vote badly, applied now to the Sahel's coups and the migration corridors. Recall block. The synthesis sentence? Geography deals, history shapes institutions, institutions play. The resource curse's three mechanisms? Dutch disease, volatility, and rents replacing consent. The middle-income trap bites when? Cheap labor loses before innovation arrives. Aid's clearest wins? Health — smallpox, PEPFAR, vaccines — and the Green Revolution. The randomistas' move? Stop asking whether aid works; test which — trials with counterfactuals. Next: reading the economic headlines — jobs day, inflation prints, and trade numbers, decoded at speed. Thanks for listening.
