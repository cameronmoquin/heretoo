Revolutions and Empires, 1789 to 1914

Transcript

Episode sixteen: revolutions and empires, 1789 to 1914 — the long nineteenth century. Review; out loud. Westphalia, year and invention? 1648, sovereignty. The Declaration channels which philosopher? Locke. Tordesillas explains what modern fact? Portuguese-speaking Brazil. Three questions. One: why did the French Revolution, unlike the American, eat itself? Two: what did the Congress of Vienna build, and how long did it hold? Three: why does a wave of revolutions in 1848 fail everywhere, and what fills the vacuum? Here we go. France, 1789. The crown is bankrupt — partly from funding the American Revolution, an irony worth savoring — and summons the Estates-General for the first time since 1614. The Third Estate declares itself a National Assembly; the Bastille falls on July fourteenth; the Declaration of the Rights of Man proclaims liberty, equality, fraternity. So far it rhymes with 1776. Then it doesn't. Question one: the differences that made the difference. America revolted against a distant crown and kept its local institutions; France had to demolish and rebuild everything at once — church, aristocracy, monarchy — with foreign armies massing on its borders. War radicalizes it: the king is guillotined in 1793, and the Committee of Public Safety under Robespierre runs the Terror — tens of thousands executed until the Terror consumes Robespierre himself in 1794. The lesson the test extracts: revolutions under siege devour their moderates. From the wreckage rises the general: Napoleon Bonaparte, First Consul by coup in 1799, Emperor by 1804. His export version of the Revolution — the Napoleonic Code, legal equality, careers open to talent — spreads across a conquered Europe, and the conquests seed the very nationalism that will unmake his successors. Two American connections: the Louisiana Purchase, 1803, is Napoleon liquidating his American ambitions after Haiti's revolution — the only successful slave revolt in history, independent 1804 — and the War of 1812 is a sideshow of his wars. Russia breaks him in 1812; Waterloo finishes him in 1815. Question two: the victors gather at the Congress of Vienna, 1815, under Austria's Metternich, and build the Concert of Europe on two principles: legitimacy — put the old crowns back — and balance of power — no state strong enough to attempt Napoleon's feat again. It is reactionary, anti-liberal, anti-national — and it works, on its own terms: no continent-wide war for ninety-nine years, 1815 to 1914. When diplomats praise or damn great-power concert diplomacy, Vienna is the reference. Midpoint check. Why did Napoleon sell Louisiana? Haiti's successful revolt destroyed the French Caribbean project Louisiana was meant to feed, and war with Britain loomed — so he cashed out for fifteen million. But the lid Vienna screwed down kept rattling: liberalism and nationalism, the Revolution's orphans. Greece breaks free of the Ottomans in the 1820s; France has another revolution in 1830; and then question three — 1848, the Springtime of Peoples: revolutions in Paris, Vienna, Berlin, Budapest, Rome, all within months. All fail. Why? The revolutionaries split — liberals wanted constitutions, workers wanted bread, nationalists wanted borders, and the armies stayed loyal to the crowns. The same year, Marx and Engels publish the Communist Manifesto — and Seneca Falls holds America's first women's rights convention; 1848 is a heavy anchor year. What fills the vacuum after idealism fails is realpolitik: unification achieved not by parliaments but by blood and iron. Count Cavour maneuvers Italy together by 1861; Otto von Bismarck forges Germany through three short wars — Denmark, Austria, then France in 1870 — proclaiming the German Empire at Versailles in 1871, in France's own palace, a humiliation France will not forget. Germany's arrival as the strongest state on the continent is the crack in Vienna's balance that 1914 will split open. The rest of the century belongs to empire and its resisters, in one sweep. Britain industrializes first — railways, factories, the world's banker — and after crushing the 1857 Indian rebellion rules India directly, the Raj, jewel of an empire covering a quarter of the earth. The Opium Wars, 1839 and 1856, force China open; unequal treaties, Hong Kong ceded, and the Taiping Rebellion — a pseudo-Christian civil war killing perhaps twenty million — leave the Qing dynasty a shell that finally collapses in 1911, Sun Yat-sen's republic replacing it. Japan, alone, reverse-engineers the West: the Meiji Restoration, 1868 — constitution, conscription, industry — and then proves the model by beating China in 1895 and, shockingly, Russia in 1905, the first modern defeat of a European power by an Asian one. Africa: the Berlin Conference, 1884 to 85, sets the rules and the Scramble carves the continent — by 1914 only Ethiopia, which crushed an Italian army at Adwa in 1896, and Liberia remain independent, and the borders drawn in Berlin, indifferent to language and nation, are the frozen conflicts and civil wars of episode twenty. Latin America: Bolívar and San Martín liberate the south from Spain by the 1820s — which is precisely the moment the Monroe Doctrine appears, warning Europe off the new republics. And Russia frees its serfs in 1861 — the same year America's Civil War begins over its own unfree labor; the test loves that pairing. Recall block. Vienna's two principles? Legitimacy and balance of power — and the peace held ninety-nine years. Why did 1848 fail everywhere? Liberals, workers, and nationalists split; the armies stayed loyal. Who unified Germany, by what method, proclaimed where? Bismarck; blood and iron — three wars; Versailles, 1871. Meiji Japan proved itself by defeating whom in 1905? Russia. The two African states never colonized? Ethiopia and Liberia. 1861, both hemispheres? Russian serfs freed; the American Civil War begins; Italy unified, for the triple. Next: the world breaks — two wars, an armistice masquerading as peace, and the empires' end. Thanks for listening.
