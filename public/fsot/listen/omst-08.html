OMST episode eight: reading comprehension, drilled by ear. A warning about the format first, because it matters. On the real test you will have the passage in front of you and can reread any line. Here you cannot, so listening is harder than reading will be. That is deliberate: if you can hold a passage's structure in your head from hearing it twice, holding it with the text in front of you is easy. Each passage is read twice, slowly, then the questions. Answer out loud. Method reminder, because you are about to use it. Stem first when you can, structure over detail, main idea in your own words, then the specific line, then eliminate the overstatements and the outside facts. Here we go. Passage one. Listen for the turn. The city council's proposal to convert the riverside road into a pedestrian promenade has been presented as a choice between commerce and quality of life. Local merchants predict ruin; planners cite a dozen European cities where footfall rose after cars were removed. Both sides are arguing from the same weak evidence. The European comparisons involve dense historic centers with rail access, and the riverside road serves a district where four in five visitors arrive by car. The proposal may still be right, but not for the reasons offered. Again. The city council's proposal to convert the riverside road into a pedestrian promenade has been presented as a choice between commerce and quality of life. Local merchants predict ruin; planners cite a dozen European cities where footfall rose after cars were removed. Both sides are arguing from the same weak evidence. The European comparisons involve dense historic centers with rail access, and the riverside road serves a district where four in five visitors arrive by car. The proposal may still be right, but not for the reasons offered. Question one: what is the main idea? That both sides of the debate are relying on evidence that does not fit this case. Not that the promenade is a bad idea; the passage explicitly declines to say that. The last sentence is the whole answer, and it is the sort of measured conclusion that beats the dramatic options offered beside it. Question two: why does the author mention that four in five visitors arrive by car? To show that the European comparisons do not transfer, because those centers had rail access and this district does not. It is the disanalogy, and it is doing the work of the argument. Question three: which would the author most likely consider relevant evidence? Data from a comparable car-dependent district that pedestrianized. The author's whole complaint is about comparability, so the fix is a comparison that matches on the feature that differs. Passage two. This one is a workplace document; listen for what it requires of you. All requests for after-hours building access must be submitted through the duty officer by sixteen hundred on the day access is needed. Requests submitted after that hour will be considered only where the requester can document an operational necessity that arose after the deadline. Approval is not automatic in either case. Staff holding standing after-hours access are reminded that the standing authorization covers only the spaces listed on it, and does not extend to controlled areas, which require separate approval each time. Question four: a staff member with standing after-hours access wants to enter a controlled area at nine in the evening. What does the passage say they need? Separate approval for that occasion, because the standing authorization covers only the listed spaces and never extends to controlled areas. The standing access is the trap; it sounds like it settles the question and the passage says the opposite. Question five: a request submitted at seventeen hundred with a documented necessity that arose at sixteen thirty will be what? Considered, but not necessarily approved. The passage says late requests are considered where the necessity is documented and arose after the deadline, and then says plainly that approval is not automatic in either case. Two options on a real test would be granted and refused, and both would be wrong. Passage three, the last, and this one has an author with a view. The word efficiency entered office life through the factory, and it brought the factory's assumptions with it. On a production line, the unit of work is visible and countable, so doing more per hour is unambiguously better. Very little of an office works that way. A meeting that ends early may have been efficient or may have deferred the disagreement it existed to resolve. A memo written in ten minutes may have saved fifty or cost a week. The word efficiency entered office life through the factory, and it brought the factory's assumptions with it. On a production line, the unit of work is visible and countable, so doing more per hour is unambiguously better. Very little of an office works that way. A meeting that ends early may have been efficient or may have deferred the disagreement it existed to resolve. A memo written in ten minutes may have saved fifty or cost a week. We keep the word because we have no better one, and we keep forgetting that it was borrowed. Question six: what is the author's main claim? That efficiency is a borrowed industrial concept that fits office work poorly, because office output is not countable in the way factory output is. Question seven: the author's attitude is best described how? Critical of the term's use but resigned to it, since the passage says we keep the word because we have no better one. Not hostile, not nostalgic, not neutral. Attitude questions usually land on the milder, mixed option, and here the mixture is stated outright in the final sentence. Question eight, the hardest: what can be inferred about the short meeting example? That the same observable fact, a meeting ending early, is consistent with two opposite evaluations, so the observation alone does not tell you whether it was efficient. Notice what is not inferable: that short meetings are usually bad. The passage offers the example to show ambiguity, not to make a claim about frequency, and the option that converts an example into a generalization is the classic trap. Recall block. What was the disanalogy in passage one? Rail access versus car dependence; the comparison did not transfer. What does standing after-hours access not cover? Controlled areas, which need separate approval every time. Considered is not the same as? Approved. When an example is offered to show ambiguity, what must you not do with it? Turn it into a generalization about what usually happens. Next episode, the last: the dress rehearsal and the plan for the days you have left. Thanks for listening.
