Transcript

Episode seventeen: the world wars and the empires' end, 1914 to 1991. Review; out loud. Vienna's peace lasted until? 1914 — ninety-nine years. Bismarck proclaimed the German Empire where? Versailles, 1871. The Berlin Conference did what? Set the rules for carving Africa. Three questions. One: how does one assassination consume a continent in six weeks? Two: why did Versailles produce another war within twenty years? Three: how did the European empires, victorious in 1945, vanish within a generation? Here we go. Question one is about machinery. By 1914 Europe is two armed blocs: the Triple Entente — France, Russia, Britain — facing Germany and Austria-Hungary. The alliance system means any spark jumps the firebreaks; the mobilization timetables mean nobody can pause without losing. June 28, 1914: Gavrilo Princip, a Serbian nationalist, shoots Archduke Franz Ferdinand in Sarajevo. Austria issues an ultimatum to Serbia; Russia backs Serbia; Germany backs Austria and, following its Schlieffen Plan, attacks France through neutral Belgium — which brings Britain in. Six weeks, assassination to world war, no one intending it: the test's standing lesson on escalation. The war itself: trenches from the Channel to Switzerland, Verdun and the Somme each consuming a million casualties for meters of mud, machine guns and gas outrunning tactics. 1917 turns it — Germany's unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram pull America in, and Russia exits through two revolutions: February topples the tsar, October is Lenin's Bolshevik coup, and the treaty of Brest-Litovsk buys peace at any price. American weight decides 1918; armistice on November eleventh. Sixteen million dead — and the influenza pandemic kills perhaps three times more. Four empires are simply gone: German, Austro-Hungarian, Russian, Ottoman — the last carved by the Sykes-Picot line and the mandate system into the modern Middle East, with the 1917 Balfour Declaration promising a Jewish national home in Palestine: file both for episode twenty. Question two: Versailles, 1919. The war-guilt clause pins everything on Germany; reparations bleed it; the Rhineland is stripped, the colonies taken, the army capped. Keynes — remember him from the economics lessons — walks out predicting the peace will ruin Europe. Weimar Germany's hyperinflation of 1923, wheelbarrows of marks, sears the humiliation in. Then the Depression arrives, and unemployment does what inflation started: Hitler, whose Beer Hall Putsch failed in 1923, wins power legally in January 1933. The pattern is general — Mussolini's Fascists had marched on Rome in 1922; militarists captured Japan, seizing Manchuria in 1931. The democracies appease: the Rhineland remilitarized 1936, Austria annexed March 1938, and at Munich, September 1938, Britain and France hand over the Sudetenland for Chamberlain's peace for our time. Munich becomes diplomacy's most abused analogy — every hawk's word for concession. The Nazi-Soviet Pact of August 1939 splits Poland on paper; September first, Germany invades, and the war is on. Midpoint check. Why does Versailles fail where Vienna succeeded? Vienna reintegrated the defeated power into the balance; Versailles punished and excluded Germany while leaving it strong enough to seek revenge — grievance plus capability. The Second World War in one arc: blitzkrieg takes Poland, then France in six weeks of 1940; Britain holds alone through the Battle of Britain; June 1941, Hitler invades the Soviet Union, the war's true meat grinder — the Eastern Front kills more than all other theaters combined, and the Soviet Union loses twenty-seven million people, the fact that still shapes Russian memory. December 1941, Pearl Harbor brings America in. The hinges you know from episode four: Midway, Stalingrad — the entire German Sixth Army surrendered, the tide's turn — D-Day, and the bombs on Hiroshima and Nagasaki, August 1945. And beneath the war, the Holocaust: the Nazi state's industrial murder of six million Jews, with millions of others — the crime that gives the postwar order its vocabulary: genocide, coined 1944; the Nuremberg trials, individual accountability for crimes against humanity; the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948; the Genocide Convention, the same year. Remember the architecture from episode fourteen — the UN, Bretton Woods — as the same generation's answer to the same catastrophe. Question three: the empires dissolve, and the answer is that the war broke them — bankrupted the metropoles, shattered the myth of European invincibility in Asia, and armed the colonized with the victors' own rhetoric of self-determination. The chain: India first, 1947 — Gandhi's nonviolent campaigns deliver independence, but partition into India and Pakistan kills perhaps a million in communal violence and seeds three wars and the Kashmir dispute, still live. Israel, 1948 — the UN partition of the Palestine mandate; the Arab states reject it and lose the first war; the Palestinian refugee question is born. China, 1949 — Mao's Communists win the civil war; the Nationalists retreat to Taiwan, creating the strait question that anchors episode nineteen. Indonesia takes independence from the Dutch; Vietnam fights France to defeat at Dien Bien Phu, 1954, splitting at the seventeenth parallel and handing America a war. Suez, 1956, is the empire's obituary: Britain and France invade Egypt over the nationalized canal, and America — their own ally — forces them out; the imperial powers now ask permission. 1960 is the Year of Africa, seventeen new states; Algeria bleeds free of France in 1962; the Non-Aligned Movement — Nehru, Nasser, Tito — refuses both blocs. By 1975, with Portugal's African empire the last to fall, the European empires that ruled the map in 1914 exist only in the borders they left behind. The Cold War's end you already know from episode five: the Wall in 1989, the Soviet flag down on Christmas 1991 — which closed Europe's short, terrible twentieth century, 1914 to 1991, war to war to wall. Recall block. The 1914 escalation chain, compressed? Assassination, ultimatum, alliances trigger, timetables lock — six weeks to world war. Munich now means? Appeasement — concession that feeds the appetite it means to sate. Stalingrad mattered because? A whole German army surrendered — the Eastern tide turned. 1947, 1948, 1949 — the decolonization drumbeat? India partitioned; Israel born; Mao wins China. What did Suez 1956 prove? The imperial powers now needed American permission — the empires were over. Next: a rapid-fire logic gauntlet — fifteen arguments, name the flaw at speed. Thanks for listening.
