The Road to the Civil War, 1820 to 1861

Episode two: the road to the Civil War, 1820 to 1861. First, sixty seconds on last episode; answer out loud in the pauses. The five presidents of the Founders' Republic, in order? Washington, Adams, Jefferson, Madison, Monroe. Which case established judicial review, and in what year? Marbury versus Madison, 1803. What idea did the Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions plant? That states could judge federal laws unconstitutional and refuse them. Watch that idea grow teeth today. Now, three questions this episode will answer. One: which single law repealed the Missouri Compromise line, and what new party did it create? Two: why did the Compromise of 1850, designed to cool the country down, heat it up instead? Three: what did the Supreme Court do in 1857 that made compromise legally impossible? This era has one tension: slavery in the territories. Not slavery where it already existed, but slavery in the new lands. Every crisis in the next forty years is that question wearing a different costume, and every deal undoes the last. Think of it as a staircase going down, and count the steps with me. Step one, 1820, where we left off: the Missouri Compromise. Missouri enters slave, Maine enters free, and the thirty-six thirty line divides the Louisiana territory. Henry Clay brokers it, earning the name the Great Compromiser. The balance in the Senate is preserved, and everyone pretends the question is settled. Meanwhile the Jacksonian era arrives. The 1824 election has four candidates; nobody wins a majority; the House picks John Quincy Adams after Clay throws him support and becomes Secretary of State. Jackson's people call it the corrupt bargain, and in 1828 Jackson wins the rematch in a landslide, powered by newly enfranchised white male voters without property. Jacksonian democracy: the common man, the spoils system, and a deep hatred of concentrated power, except his own. Two Jackson stories matter for the test. First, the Bank War: Jackson vetoes the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States, pulls the deposits, and kills it. The Panic of 1837 follows, landing on his successor Van Buren. Second, Indian removal: the Indian Removal Act of 1830, and when the Supreme Court rules in Worcester versus Georgia that the Cherokee are a sovereign nation Georgia cannot touch, Jackson ignores the ruling. The Trail of Tears follows in 1838, four thousand Cherokee dead on the march to Oklahoma. And here is step two on our staircase, 1832, the Nullification Crisis. South Carolina, furious over high tariffs, declares the federal tariff null and void within its borders, invoking exactly the Virginia and Kentucky idea from episode one. Jackson threatens to hang his own vice president, Calhoun, and Congress passes a Force Bill. Clay compromises again, the tariff comes down, South Carolina backs off. But notice: a state has now rehearsed defying the Union, and the word nullification is loaded for 1861. The 1840s bring expansion, and expansion brings the slavery question roaring back. Texas, independent from Mexico since 1836, remember the Alamo, is annexed in 1845 under Tyler. James K. Polk then takes the country to war with Mexico in 1846, and by 1848 the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo hands over the Mexican Cession: California, Nevada, Utah, Arizona, New Mexico. Manifest Destiny fulfilled. Polk also splits Oregon with Britain at the forty-ninth parallel. But the moment the new land arrives, step three: the Wilmot Proviso, a proposal to ban slavery in everything taken from Mexico. It fails, repeatedly, but it forces every politician to pick a side, and it splits the parties along sectional lines. Step four, the Compromise of 1850, Clay's last deal, pushed through by Stephen Douglas. California enters free, which breaks the Senate balance forever. In exchange, the South gets a harsher Fugitive Slave Act, forcing Northern citizens to help catch escaped slaves. That law radicalizes the North; Harriet Beecher Stowe answers it with Uncle Tom's Cabin in 1852, and Northern juries refuse to convict rescuers. A deal meant to cool things down heats them up. Midpoint check. Why did the Fugitive Slave Act radicalize the North? Because it conscripted ordinary Northerners into slavery's enforcement. A Northerner could ignore slavery in Georgia; he could not ignore a marshal ordering him to help catch a man on his own street. The compromise made slavery personal to people it had never touched. Step five, 1854, the one to know cold: the Kansas-Nebraska Act. Douglas, wanting a railroad and Southern votes, proposes organizing Kansas and Nebraska under popular sovereignty, let the settlers vote on slavery. But both territories sit north of thirty-six thirty. The act repeals the Missouri Compromise line outright. The North explodes. The Whig party dies, and a new party is born that year to oppose slavery's expansion: the Republicans. Popular sovereignty gets its test immediately, and fails in blood. Pro-slavery and anti-slavery settlers race into Kansas, set up rival governments, and start killing each other: Bleeding Kansas, 1856. The violence reaches the Senate floor when Congressman Preston Brooks canes Senator Charles Sumner nearly to death at his desk. Step six, 1857: Dred Scott versus Sandford. Chief Justice Taney rules that Black Americans are not citizens and cannot sue, and, further, that Congress never had the power to ban slavery in any territory. The Missouri Compromise, already repealed, is now declared to have been unconstitutional all along. There is no longer any legal way to contain slavery's spread. The Republican party's entire platform has just been ruled out of bounds. 1858: the Lincoln-Douglas debates, seven of them, for an Illinois Senate seat. Lincoln loses the seat but becomes a national figure, and he traps Douglas into the Freeport Doctrine, that settlers could still keep slavery out by refusing to protect it, which destroys Douglas with the South. 1859: John Brown raids the federal arsenal at Harpers Ferry, hoping to arm a slave revolt. He hangs, the North makes him a martyr, and the South concludes the North wants race war. The last step, 1860. The Democrats split North and South; Lincoln wins with about forty percent of the popular vote and not a single Southern electoral vote. For the Deep South, the mere election of a president opposed to slavery's expansion is enough. South Carolina, of course South Carolina, secedes in December 1860. Six more states follow, forming the Confederacy under Jefferson Davis. Buchanan watches and does nothing. And on April 12, 1861, Confederate guns open fire on Fort Sumter in Charleston harbor. The staircase has reached the bottom. Recall time. Answer out loud in the pause. What did the Kansas-Nebraska Act repeal, and what party was born in response? It repealed the Missouri Compromise line, and the Republican party was born to oppose slavery's expansion. What did Dred Scott decide about Congress and the territories? That Congress had no power to ban slavery in any territory, so no legal containment was left. Put these in order: Compromise of 1850, Wilmot Proviso, Kansas-Nebraska, Missouri Compromise. Missouri Compromise, 1820. Wilmot Proviso, 1846. Compromise of 1850. Kansas-Nebraska, 1854. Which crisis rehearsed secession in 1832, and over what? The Nullification Crisis, over tariffs. Next episode: the war itself, Reconstruction's rise and abandonment, and the Gilded Age. Thanks for listening.
