Regional deep dive five: Latin America — the hemisphere Monroe claimed, the interventions that claim justified, and the neighborhood's files today. Warm-up; out loud. Africa's three inheritances? Frozen borders, extraction states, commodity economies. The five anchors? Nigeria, Ethiopia, South Africa, DRC, Egypt. Djibouti matters because? Bab el-Mandeb — everyone's base watches the strait. Three questions. One: why did Latin America's independence produce caudillos instead of Madisons? Two: what did a century of American intervention actually build and break? Three: what are the hemisphere's five files today? Here we go. Question one: the liberators won and the institutions didn't. Bolívar in the north, San Martín in the south freed Spanish America by the 1820s — remember the Monroe timing — and Brazil exited Portugal almost bloodlessly under its own emperor. But three centuries of Spanish rule had built extraction, not self-government: no colonial assemblies like North America's, remember salutary neglect — its absence here; society stratified by caste — peninsulares over creoles over mestizos over Indians and enslaved Africans; wealth in land, the haciendas, and silver. Bolívar himself despaired — I have plowed the sea. Into the institutional vacuum rode the caudillos, strongmen on horseback trading order for power, and the pattern set: constitutions plentiful, constitutionalism scarce; the army as politics' referee; liberal-conservative civil wars over church and federalism. The exceptions prove the diagnosis: Chile and Costa Rica, with earlier institutional settlements, became the region's stability stories — Costa Rica abolishing its army outright in 1948. Add the export-boom dependency: guano, nitrates, coffee, beef, copper — commodity economies wired to Europe and later the States, the structure Raúl Prebisch's dependency school named in the 1950s: the periphery sells cheap and buys dear — the intellectual root of import substitution, the region's mid-century strategy of tariff-walled home industry, which built factories and bottlenecks alike and died in the 1980s debt crisis: the lost decade, defaults and hyperinflation — Argentina's serial drama — ending in the market reforms of the Washington Consensus, itself resented as the next dictation. That whiplash — dependency, protection, austerity — is the region's economic memory; when Latin American diplomats bristle at conditionality, remember IMF episode fourteen, this is the file. Question two: the interventions. The ledger, rapid but honest. Cuba leashed by Platt; Panama taken — remember TR — the Canal Zone an American strip through a country until Carter's treaties returned it; the Roosevelt Corollary cashed in Marine landings — Haiti, Nicaragua, the Dominican Republic, occupied for years, customs houses run from Washington; the Good Neighbor pause; then the Cold War chapters: Guatemala, 1954 — the CIA topples elected Árbenz for land reform touching United Fruit, and the civil war that follows kills two hundred thousand, mostly Maya; Cuba, 1959 — Castro's revolution, the Bay of Pigs failure, the Missile Crisis, and an embargo now generations old; Chile, 1973 — Allende elected, destabilized, and dead in Pinochet's coup on September eleventh — Chileans note the date — with Chicago-school economics imposed at bayonet: the region's most cited case of democracy sacrificed to anticommunism; Argentina's junta and the Dirty War's thirty thousand disappeared, the Mothers of the Plaza circling; Operation Condor networking the dictatorships' assassins across borders; Central America's 1980s — Contras armed against Sandinista Nicaragua, remember Iran-Contra; El Salvador's death squads, Archbishop Romero shot at the altar. What it built: compliant governments, for a time, and a deep reflex of anti-yanqui nationalism — the reason Latin capitals hedge toward autonomy today. What it broke: institutions, repeatedly, in exactly the countries whose weakness later exported instability north. The redemocratization wave of the 1980s and 90s closed the dictatorships; the truth commissions counted the cost. Midpoint check. Guatemala 1954 and Chile 1973 share what structure? Elected reformers, property touched, coups assisted, decades of repression after — anticommunism spending democracy to buy stability it didn't get. Question three: the five files on today's desk. File one: migration — the corridors north from Venezuela's collapse, nearly eight million out, remember; from Central America's Northern Triangle — gangs, climate-stressed coffee, weak states; managed through border externalization deals; remittances meanwhile propping the senders' economies — a quarter of GDP for Honduras and El Salvador, the geography episode's number. File two: drugs and crime — cocaine's supply chain from Andean leaf through Mexican cartels, fentanyl's precursors transshipped, and the militarized whack-a-mole: Colombia's long war ending in the 2016 FARC peace — imperfect, Nobel-crowned; Mexico's cartel violence at civil-war scale; Ecuador's ports captured by gangs; Bukele's El Salvador mass-incarceration model — homicide crushed, due process with it — the region's most copied and most contested experiment. File three: the pink and blue tides — politics swinging left and right in synchronized waves: Brazil's Lula and Bolsonaro trading power, Argentina's Milei slashing with a chainsaw prop, Mexico's dominant-party morena era; the institutions mostly holding, the polarization familiar to any American observer. File four: China — now South America's top trading partner: soy, copper, lithium out; infrastructure and 5G in; the space-tracking station in Patagonia the Pentagon cites; Washington rediscovering its neighborhood late — the Monroe ghost in modern dress, episode twenty's phrase. File five: the anchors' own weight — Brazil as climate great power, the Amazon's fate a global file and a sovereignty flashpoint; Mexico as the manufacturing nearshore winner, top American trade partner, USMCA's other half; both insisting on multipolar options. The test's closing sentence: the hemisphere's problems are shared by design — migration, drugs, supply chains — so the policy question is never whether to engage the neighbors, only how. Recall block. Bolívar's despair explains? Institutions missing at birth — caudillos filled the vacuum. Import substitution was, and died how? Tariff-walled home industry; the eighties debt crisis killed it. The two September elevenths? 2001 New York; 1973 Santiago — Pinochet's coup. The 2016 Colombian peace ended? The FARC war — the hemisphere's longest insurgency. China's role now? South America's top trading partner — commodities out, infrastructure in. Next: the logic bootcamps — assumptions first. Thanks for listening.
