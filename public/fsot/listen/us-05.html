Transcript

Episode five: the Cold War and after, 1945 to now. Review first; answer in the pauses. The four Progressive amendments? Income tax, direct election of senators, Prohibition, women's suffrage; sixteen through nineteen. Why did the New Deal survive the Supreme Court? The 1937 switch in time; the Court began upholding, and the commerce power stretched through Wickard. What did Lend-Lease do, and when? March 1941; lent Britain war materiel before America entered the war. Three questions for this episode. One: what single idea organized forty-five years of American foreign policy, and who wrote it down first? Two: why did the civil rights movement finally break through in the mid-1960s after seventy years of Jim Crow? Three: what do Truman in Korea and the War Powers Act have to do with each other? Here we go. The answer to question one is containment, and the author is George Kennan, a Foreign Service Officer, your future colleague, whose Long Telegram from Moscow in 1946 argued the Soviet system was expansionist but patient, and could be checked by firm, patient counter-pressure everywhere it pushed. That one idea becomes policy in 1947, the hinge year: the Truman Doctrine, aid to Greece and Turkey, support for free peoples resisting subjugation; the Marshall Plan, thirteen billion dollars to rebuild Western Europe, because desperate economies vote communist; and the National Security Act, creating the CIA, the NSC, and the Defense Department, the machinery of a permanent rivalry. Nineteen forty-seven is the most test-dense year in the century; when in doubt on a Cold War founding date, guess it. The pattern of the early Cold War: probe and counter. Stalin blockades Berlin in 1948; the answer is the Berlin Airlift, a year of supply flights rather than a shot fired. NATO forms in 1949, permanent alliance at last, Washington's Farewell finally retired. The same year, two shocks: the Soviets test a bomb, and China goes communist under Mao. In 1950, North Korea invades South Korea. Truman commits troops under a UN flag without asking Congress for a declaration of war, hold that thought, it's question three. The war seesaws: MacArthur's Inchon landing, China floods across the Yalu, MacArthur publicly demands escalation and Truman fires him, civilian control of the military reaffirmed. Armistice at the thirty-eighth parallel, 1953, where it started, and where it still sits. At home, fear curdles into McCarthyism, the senator waving lists of imaginary communists until the Army hearings of 1954 break him on television. Eisenhower presides over boom years: the Interstate Highway System, born partly as defense infrastructure, and in 1957 he sends the 101st Airborne into Little Rock to walk nine Black students into a high school, the first federal troops in the South since 1877. Which brings us to question two: why does civil rights break through now? Stack the causes. The legal track: the NAACP's long campaign peaks in Brown versus Board, 1954, Thurgood Marshall arguing, separate is inherently unequal, Plessy overruled. The movement track: Montgomery, 1955, Rosa Parks and a boycott that makes Martin Luther King national; sit-ins; Freedom Rides; Birmingham's fire hoses on children, televised. That's the third cause: television, the movement's suffering made visible in every living room, including overseas, where Jim Crow is Soviet propaganda gold in the contest for the new nations of Africa and Asia. Moral pressure, legal groundwork, and Cold War embarrassment converge, and after Kennedy's assassination Lyndon Johnson, a Southerner who knows where every Senate body is buried, muscles through the Civil Rights Act of 1964, public accommodations and employment, and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, the Fifteenth Amendment finally enforced, ninety-five years late. Midpoint check. Put these in order: Cuban Missile Crisis, Bay of Pigs, Castro takes Havana, Berlin Wall goes up. Castro 1959, Bay of Pigs April 1961, the Wall August 1961, and the Missile Crisis October 1962, thirteen days at the edge, ended by a public Soviet withdrawal and a quiet American promise on Turkish missiles. Anything Cuba is 1959 to 62; anything Berlin is 1948, 1961, or 1989. Then Vietnam, containment's overreach. The Gulf of Tonkin Resolution, 1964, a blank check on a murky incident; half a million troops by 1968; the Tet Offensive, a military defeat for the North but a political catastrophe for Johnson, because it made the official optimism a lie. Johnson quits the race; Nixon wins promising peace with honor, and here's the era's great irony: the red-baiter goes to Beijing in 1972, splitting China from Moscow, and signs SALT I with the Soviets, détente. Paris accords 1973, Saigon falls 1975. And Congress, burned by two undeclared wars, passes the War Powers Act over Nixon's veto in 1973, sixty days of military action, then congressional approval or withdrawal. That's the Truman-to-War-Powers thread of question three: Korea began the era of presidential war, and War Powers was Congress's attempt, still contested every time it's invoked, to take the power back. Nixon's own presidency dies by cover-up: Watergate, the tapes, United States versus Nixon, unanimous, the president must comply with subpoenas, resignation August 1974. Ford pardons him; Carter inherits stagflation, inflation plus stagnation, which isn't supposed to be possible, brokers the Camp David Accords between Egypt and Israel, and is broken by 1979: the Iranian revolution and hostages, and the Soviet invasion of Afghanistan. Reagan bets that the Soviet system cannot afford an arms race: military buildup, SDI, tax cuts, and in his second term a partner, Gorbachev, whose glasnost and perestroika loosen what force had held. The INF Treaty of 1987 eliminates a whole missile class. Then the dominoes: the Wall opens November 1989, Germany reunifies, and on Christmas Day 1991 the Soviet Union votes itself out of existence. Containment, the patient idea from a diplomat's telegram, outlasted its adversary without the great war everyone expected. After that, fast forward. Bush 41: the Gulf War, a real coalition, Kuwait freed in a hundred hours. Clinton: NAFTA, budget surpluses, and impeachment over the Lewinsky affair, acquitted. Bush 43: September 11, 2001, al-Qaeda kills three thousand; Afghanistan invaded; the Patriot Act; then Iraq 2003 on weapons that weren't found. Two thousand eight: the financial crisis, housing leverage collapsing into the worst slump since 1929, answered by bailouts and stimulus; Obama, the first Black president, signs the Affordable Care Act in 2010. Trump: tariffs and trade war with China. Biden: pandemic recovery, the Afghanistan withdrawal of 2021, and rearming Ukraine. The through-line since 1991: no single organizing idea has replaced containment, and the era's question, terrorism, then China, then democracy itself, is still open. Recall block. Who authored containment, and in what document? George Kennan, the Long Telegram, 1946. Name the three creations of 1947. Truman Doctrine, Marshall Plan, National Security Act, plus the CIA, NSC, and Defense Department inside it. Civil Rights Act versus Voting Rights Act? Sixty-four, public accommodations and employment; sixty-five, the vote. What did the War Powers Act of 1973 require? Congressional approval within sixty days of committing troops, or withdrawal; passed over Nixon's veto. That completes the American spine, 1789 to now. Next in the series: logic drills, the think-aloud way. Thanks for listening.
