Chokepoints, Capitals, and Traps

Transcript

Episode twenty-four: geography that scores — chokepoints, capitals, and the traps the test sets. Review; out loud. The trilemma's corners? Fixed rate, free capital, independent policy — pick two. Quotas are worse than tariffs because? No revenue collected — the scarcity premium goes private. The reserve currency grants America? Cheap borrowing and sanctions leverage — the exorbitant privilege. Three questions. One: what are the eight chokepoints, west to east, and what does each connect? Two: which capitals are not their country's famous city — the distractor list? Three: which countries are landlocked, and why does the test care? Here we go. Question one: the chokepoints — the straits and canals where world trade squeezes through, each a potential fist around a windpipe. Walk them west to east, and give each its two waters and one headline. Panama: Atlantic to Pacific by canal — American-built, returned to Panama at the end of 1999 under the Torrijos-Carter treaties; drought-limited transits are its modern story. Gibraltar: Atlantic to Mediterranean — Britain's rock on Spain's toe. The Bosporus: the Black Sea's only door, through Istanbul — governed by the Montreux Convention, which lets Turkey close it to warships in wartime; every grain ship out of Ukraine and every Russian warship into the Mediterranean cares. Suez: Mediterranean to Red Sea — Egyptian since Nasser nationalized it in 1956, remember the crisis; a single stuck container ship in 2021 taxed world trade for a week. Bab el-Mandeb: Red Sea to Gulf of Aden — the southern exit of the Suez route, Yemen on one side, Djibouti on the other, which is why every navy keeps a base in tiny Djibouti and why Houthi missiles could reroute world shipping around Africa. Hormuz: Persian Gulf to Gulf of Oman — a fifth of the world's oil through twenty-one nautical miles, Iran on the north shore; the chokepoint of chokepoints. Malacca: Indian Ocean to South China Sea, past Singapore — China's energy lifeline, and China's Malacca dilemma is that the US Navy could close it; Belt and Road pipelines exist partly to route around it. And the Taiwan Strait: the passage between China and Taiwan, world semiconductor traffic on top of everything else. The pattern that answers test questions: name the two waters, and you've located the strait; name the strait, and you've found the crisis. Midpoint check. Why does everyone garrison Djibouti? It watches Bab el-Mandeb — the Red Sea and Suez traffic's southern gate. Question two: capitals that are not the biggest city — the test's favorite distractor, because the famous city is sitting right there among the options looking plausible. The list, with the famous decoy in parentheses, and say the capital in the pause as I name the country. Australia? Canberra — not Sydney. Canada? Ottawa — not Toronto. New Zealand? Wellington — not Auckland. Brazil? Brasília — not Rio, a purpose-built capital of 1960. Nigeria? Abuja — not Lagos, moved inland in 1991. Turkey? Ankara — not Istanbul, Atatürk's deliberate break with the imperial city. Pakistan? Islamabad — not Karachi. Morocco? Rabat — not Casablanca. Switzerland? Bern — not Zurich or Geneva. South Africa runs three: Pretoria executive, Cape Town legislative, Bloemfontein judicial. Tanzania? Dodoma, not Dar es Salaam. Myanmar? Naypyidaw, built by the junta. Kazakhstan? Astana, not Almaty. Bolivia splits Sucre and La Paz. Côte d'Ivoire? Yamoussoukro, not Abidjan. And the United States itself belongs on this list — Washington, not New York — which is the memory hook: purpose-built capitals are a global genre, and the famous commercial city is usually the wrong answer. Question three: landlocked states — no coastline, and the test cares because geography is destiny in trade costs, pipeline politics, and war. The memorable ones by region. South America has exactly two: Bolivia — which lost its coast to Chile in the War of the Pacific and still maintains a navy on Lake Titicaca in protest — and Paraguay. Europe's stack: Switzerland, Austria, Czechia, Slovakia, Hungary, Serbia, plus micro-states like Andorra; the double-trap is Liechtenstein and Uzbekistan, the world's only doubly landlocked countries — landlocked entirely by landlocked neighbors. Asia: Nepal and Bhutan in the Himalayas, Laos in Southeast Asia, Mongolia squeezed between Russia and China, Afghanistan, and all five Central Asian stans — with the asterisk that Kazakhstan and Turkmenistan touch the Caspian, a lake with sea pretensions. Africa: Ethiopia — coastal until Eritrea's 1993 independence took the shore, which is why Ethiopian port access through Djibouti is a standing diplomatic file — plus Chad, Niger, Mali, Burkina Faso, Uganda, Rwanda, Burundi, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Botswana, Malawi, and more; the Sahel coup belt from episode twenty is largely landlocked, poverty and isolation compounding. The rule the test rewards: landlocked plus resource-rich equals pipeline dependence — every export negotiates a neighbor. Recall block. The eight chokepoints, west to east, at speed. Panama, Gibraltar, Bosporus, Suez, Bab el-Mandeb, Hormuz, Malacca, Taiwan Strait. Hormuz connects? The Persian Gulf to the Gulf of Oman — a fifth of world oil. Turkey's strait treaty? Montreux — Turkey may close the Bosporus to warships in wartime. South America's two landlocked states? Bolivia and Paraguay. The doubly landlocked pair? Liechtenstein and Uzbekistan. Ethiopia lost its coast when? Eritrea's independence, 1993 — hence the Djibouti lifeline. Next: the physical and human geography layer — rivers, mountains, climate, and the demography that moves policy. Thanks for listening.
