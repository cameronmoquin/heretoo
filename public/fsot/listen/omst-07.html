OMST, Situational Judgment Drills Two

Transcript

OMST episode seven: twelve more situational judgment scenarios, in areas the first drill did not touch. Crisis, technology, travel, written communication, onboarding, and coordination across time zones. Same format: scene, silence, answer aloud, then the reasoning. The method underneath, once, fast: who owns the decision; what category; cut the rule-breakers; cut the passive; act within authority and inform; sooner beats later. One. During the workday the post's emergency alarm sounds and the announcement directs staff to a safe area. You are in the middle of formatting a cable that must transmit within the hour. What do you do? Go to the safe area immediately and take direction from the security staff. This is the safety category, and the category decides it before any other consideration is even weighed. The cable is the sort of genuinely important work that makes this a real test rather than an obvious one, and it is still not close. No document transmits on a schedule that outranks an evacuation order. Two. The Ambassador's mobile device stops receiving mail an hour before a trip. You are not an information management specialist. What do you do? Try the basic steps you are trained and authorized to perform, and contact information management immediately in parallel rather than after you have exhausted your own ideas. Notice the word parallel. The ostrich answer here is doing nothing and waiting for IM; the bulldozer answer is troubleshooting a government device beyond your authority until departure. Working the problem and raising it at the same time is the shape that fits the clock. Three. A traveler's flight is cancelled overnight and they will miss the first meeting of a two-day visit. You find out at six in the morning. What do you do? Notify the control officer and the affected offices at once, and come with options: reorder the schedule, move the meeting later, or offer a video link. Six in the morning feels early to wake people; the alternative is that they learn at nine, when the meeting is at ten and nothing can be done. Bad news travels best early, and the options are what make it a report rather than a complaint. Four. You are asked to take notes at a meeting. Partway through, the discussion turns to a personnel matter that is clearly sensitive. What do you do? Ask the chair whether they want the discussion recorded in the notes, and follow the instruction. Do not decide unilaterally to omit it, and do not record it in detail and sort it out afterward. The chair owns the record. Asking takes five seconds and puts the judgment where it belongs, which is the whole lesson of step one. Five. You draft an email for your supervisor's signature. Rereading it after sending, you realize a sentence could be read as committing the Embassy to something it has not agreed to. What do you do? Tell your supervisor immediately, and propose a short clarifying follow-up for their approval. Ambiguity in writing from an embassy is not a stylistic problem, it is a commitment problem; the recipient may act on the reading you did not intend. The self-report rule applies to what you wrote, not only to what you did. Six. A colleague three time zones away keeps proposing calls that fall in the middle of your night, and has done it three times. What do you do? Tell them directly what hours work on your end and propose a recurring slot that splits the inconvenience. Silently taking the calls builds resentment and is unsustainable; declining without an alternative makes you the obstacle. The general principle for distributed work: name your constraint and offer a structure, rather than negotiating each occurrence. Seven. A new Office Management Specialist arrives at post and asks you how things really work, including a few things that are done differently from the written procedure. What do you do? Explain the written procedure, and where local practice differs, explain the difference and why it exists. What you do not do is teach the workaround as though it were the rule. A newcomer who learns the shortcut without the rule cannot tell later which parts are safe to bend, and that is how an office drifts. Eight. You are covering the front office for a colleague on leave and a request arrives that you do not know how to handle. What do you do? Acknowledge the request, say when you will have an answer, then find out who handles it and route it. The failure mode when covering is silence: the requester assumes the desk is unattended and escalates elsewhere. Acknowledging with a time is the whole trick, and it costs one line. Nine. Two documents in the shared drive have similar names and slightly different content, and you cannot tell which is current. What do you do? Do not answer from either one until you have confirmed which is authoritative; ask the document owner, and then make the distinction visible so the next person does not face the same fork. This is the record-versus-copy problem, and answering from whichever is nearest is exactly how two officers give contradictory answers in good faith. Ten. You notice the Ambassador's schedule has them leaving an event at the same minute the next meeting begins across town. What do you do? Flag it now with a realistic travel estimate and a proposed fix. Scheduling is not arithmetic on paper; the value you add is knowing that the paper is wrong. A calendar that cannot physically be executed is a calendar that will fail publicly, and it will fail at the worst possible moment. Eleven. A journalist telephones the office and asks you to confirm whether a particular meeting took place. What do you do? Do not confirm or deny anything; refer the call to the public affairs section. Press engagement is a designated function held by people accountable for what the mission says publicly. Even confirming a meeting occurred is a statement on behalf of the United States, and a friendly, helpful tone is precisely what makes this trap effective. Twelve. You are asked to arrange an event, and you realize partway through planning that the budget approved is not going to cover it. What do you do? Stop, report the gap to whoever owns the budget with revised numbers and options to reduce scope, and get a decision before spending more. The tempting wrong answers are proceeding and sorting it out afterward, or quietly cutting corners to fit. Both convert a solvable planning problem into a financial one, and financial problems at a mission are a different category of trouble. Recall block. What category outranks everything, including urgent work? Safety, and security with it. When you are out of your depth on a device or a system, what is the shape of the right answer? Do what you are authorized to do and raise it in parallel, not afterward. What makes early bad news a report rather than a complaint? Arriving with options. Who owns the meeting record? The chair; ask rather than decide. A press call gets what response? No confirmation of anything; refer it to public affairs.
