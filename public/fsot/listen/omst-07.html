Review from last episode; out loud. What are the two reasons you miss an item, and the fix for each? Not knowing the rule, which is studying; and misreading the stem, which is slowing down at the first line. Which section do you move fast on to bank time? English expression. OMST episode seven: twelve more situational judgment scenarios, in areas the first drill did not touch. Crisis, technology, travel, written communication, onboarding, and coordination across time zones. Every scenario wants two answers, so name the worst option first and then the best. Worst separates more cleanly, because a clearly wrong answer is easier to recognise than a best answer competing with a good one. The four questions, once, fast: does it break a rule; does it act on the problem, or work around it; does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it; is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Across forty keyed items the worst answer was most often the one that works around the problem instead of acting, and breaking a rule was the rarest fault of the four. None of this requires knowing Department procedure. Scene, silence, both picks aloud. One. During the workday the post's emergency alarm sounds and the announcement directs staff to a safe area. You are in the middle of formatting a cable that must transmit within the hour. Worst: staying at the desk to make the transmission window. It breaks a rule and works around the problem in the same move. Best: go to the safe area immediately and take direction from the security staff. The cable is genuinely important work, which is what makes this a real item, and it is still not close. Two. The Ambassador's mobile device stops receiving mail an hour before a trip. You are not an information management specialist. Worst: waiting for information management to notice. It works around the problem with an hour on the clock. Best: try the basic steps you are trained and authorized to perform, and contact information management in parallel rather than after you have exhausted your own ideas. Working the problem and raising it at the same time is the shape that fits the clock. Three. A traveler's flight is cancelled overnight and they will miss the first meeting of a two-day visit. You find out at six in the morning. Worst: holding it until the office opens. Six in the morning feels early to wake people; the result is that they learn at nine, when the meeting is at ten and nothing can be done. Best: notify the control officer and the affected offices at once, and come with options: reorder the schedule, move the meeting later, or offer a video link. The options are what make it a report rather than a complaint. Four. You are asked to take notes at a meeting. Partway through, the discussion turns to a personnel matter that is clearly sensitive. Worst: deciding on your own to leave it out of the record. It goes past the only person who can settle it. Best: ask the chair whether they want the discussion recorded, and follow the instruction. The chair owns the record; asking takes five seconds and puts the judgment where it belongs. Five. You draft an email for your supervisor's signature. Rereading it after sending, you realize a sentence could be read as committing the Embassy to something it has not agreed to. Worst: leaving it and watching whether anyone reads it that way. It works around the problem, and the recipient may act on the reading you did not intend. Best: tell your supervisor immediately, and propose a short clarifying follow-up for their approval. Ambiguity in writing from an embassy is not a stylistic problem, it is a commitment problem. Six. A colleague three time zones away keeps proposing calls that fall in the middle of your night, and has done it three times. Worst: taking the calls silently. It works around the problem, builds resentment, and cannot be sustained. Declining with no alternative is the runner-up, because it hands the problem back and makes you the obstacle. Best: tell them directly what hours work on your end and propose a recurring slot that splits the inconvenience. Name your constraint and offer a structure, rather than negotiating each occurrence. Seven. A new Office Management Specialist arrives at post and asks you how things really work, including a few things that are done differently from the written procedure. Worst: teaching the workaround as though it were the rule. That installs a way around the procedure in the one person who cannot yet tell the difference. Best: explain the written procedure, and where local practice differs, explain the difference and why it exists. A newcomer who learns the shortcut without the rule cannot tell later which parts are safe to bend. Eight. You are covering the front office for a colleague on leave and a request arrives that you do not know how to handle. Worst: sitting on it until your colleague returns. The requester assumes the desk is unattended and escalates elsewhere. Best: acknowledge the request, say when you will have an answer, then find out who handles it and route it. Acknowledging with a time costs one line. Nine. Two documents in the shared drive have similar names and slightly different content, and you cannot tell which is current. Worst: answering from whichever one you opened first. It is not proportionate to what you actually know, which is that you do not know. Best: confirm with the document owner which is authoritative before you answer, then make the distinction visible so the next person does not face the same fork. Answering from whichever is nearest is exactly how two officers give contradictory answers in good faith. Ten. You notice the Ambassador's schedule has them leaving an event at the same minute the next meeting begins across town. Worst: leaving it, on the theory that whoever built the schedule knows the city. It works around a problem you can see and they cannot. Best: flag it now with a realistic travel estimate and a proposed fix. The value you add is knowing that the paper is wrong; a calendar that cannot physically be executed will fail publicly. Eleven. A journalist telephones the office and asks you to confirm whether a particular meeting took place. Worst: confirming it, because it is small and true. That is the rule-breaker, and it is rare precisely because it is dressed this well. Best: do not confirm or deny anything; refer the call to the public affairs section. Even confirming a meeting occurred is a statement on behalf of the United States, and a friendly, helpful tone is what makes this trap effective. Twelve. You are asked to arrange an event, and you realize partway through planning that the budget approved is not going to cover it. Worst: proceeding and sorting it out afterward. It works around the gap and converts a solvable planning problem into a financial one, which at a mission is a different category of trouble. Quietly cutting corners to fit is the same fault wearing thrift. Best: stop, report the gap to whoever owns the budget with revised numbers and options to reduce scope, and get a decision before spending more. Recall block. Which pick do you make first, and why? The worst, because a clearly wrong answer is easier to recognise than a best answer competing with a good one. Name the four questions. Does it break a rule; does it act on the problem, or work around it; does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it; is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Across forty keyed items, which fault was the worst answer most often? Working around the problem instead of acting on it. And which was rarest? Breaking a rule. Who owns the meeting record? The chair; ask rather than decide. A press call gets what response? No confirmation of anything; refer it to public affairs.
