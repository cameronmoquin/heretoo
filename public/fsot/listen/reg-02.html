Transcript

Regional deep dive two: the Middle East — how the Ottoman world became today's map, and the four dates that explain the present. Warm-up; out loud. The century of humiliation? 1839 to 1949. The dragons' recipe? Exports, education, land reform, state-guided credit, American platform. Article Nine? Japan's peace clause. Three questions. One: how did lines drawn in 1916 become states, and why do they strain? Two: what are the region's three revolutions of 1979, and what did each unleash? Three: why did the Arab Spring mostly fail, and what filled the failures? Here we go. Question one: for four centuries the Ottoman Empire governed the Arab lands through loose provinces and religious communities — the millet system — under a Sultan-Caliph. The First World War killed it, and the victors drew the successor map in advance: Sykes-Picot, 1916, the secret Anglo-French line splitting the Fertile Crescent; the Balfour Declaration, 1917, promising a Jewish national home in Palestine; and the McMahon correspondence promising Arabs independence for revolting — three promises, mutually incompatible, all partially kept: the region's original sin, in the standard telling. The League mandates delivered it: France took Syria and Lebanon — carving Lebanon around its Christians, a confessional state whose sectarian arithmetic still allots offices by faith; Britain took Iraq — welding three Ottoman provinces, Kurdish north, Sunni center, Shia south, into one kingdom — plus Transjordan and Palestine. Turkey alone escaped the map-makers: Atatürk expelled the invaders, abolished the caliphate in 1924, and built a secular republic by decree — script, dress, law westernized — the region's great modernization-from-above, whose secularist-versus-Islamist tension still defines Turkish politics. The strains follow the drawing: states older than their nations — Iraq's components held by force from the start; Kurds, the region's great unstated nation, perhaps forty million across four countries, promised a state at Sèvres in 1920 and denied it at Lausanne in 1923 — every Kurdish question on your test starts there. Question two: 1979 — the region's hinge year, three revolutions at once. Revolution one: Iran. The Shah — restored by the Anglo-American coup of 1953 against Mossadegh, the nationalizer of oil; remember that date, Iranians do — fell to Khomeini's Islamic Revolution: velayat-e faqih, clerical rule, the first modern theocracy, anti-American by founding myth — the embassy hostages held four hundred forty-four days. It flipped the region's alignments overnight: Iran from pillar to adversary, and Saddam's Iraq attacked in 1980 — eight years, a million casualties, chemical weapons, the war that militarized everything. Revolution two: Mecca. Militants seized the Grand Mosque, and the shaken Saudi monarchy answered by doubling down on Wahhabi orthodoxy at home and exporting it abroad — petrodollar madrassas worldwide: the conservative turn that shaped a generation of Sunni Islam. Revolution three: Afghanistan — the Soviet invasion, and the American-Saudi-Pakistani pipeline arming the mujahideen; victory in 1989, and the veterans — Bin Laden among them — founded al-Qaeda, the global jihadist current born from that war. Every file since runs through one of those three: Iran's proxy axis, Saudi-Iranian sectarian rivalry, and transnational jihadism — 1979, three times over. Midpoint check. Why do Iranians date their grievance to 1953? The CIA-MI6 coup toppled the elected Mossadegh to save the oil concession — the revolution of 1979 avenged, in its own telling, the coup of 1953. Between the revolutions and the Spring, the American wars: the Gulf War of 1991 — Saddam ejected from Kuwait by a real coalition, sanctions and containment after; then 2003 — the invasion, no weapons found, the state dissolved by decree: disbanding the Iraqi army handed trained Sunni soldiers to the insurgency, sectarian civil war followed, Iran's influence flooded the vacuum, and from the wreckage grew ISIS — declaring a caliphate across Syria and Iraq in 2014, ruling millions with videotaped terror until a global coalition broke its territory by 2019; the ideology outlived the map. File the 2003 lesson as the test files it: regime removal without a state-building plan creates the vacuum its authors feared. Question three: the Arab Spring, 2011 — a Tunisian vendor's self-immolation igniting uprisings against the police states: Tunisia's Ben Ali fell, Mubarak fell in eighteen days of Tahrir Square, Libya's Qaddafi fell to rebels and NATO jets, Yemen's Saleh negotiated out, Syria rose. Why did it mostly fail? The uprisings could topple but not govern — decades of repression had razed every institution except the army and the mosque, so the choice after the fall was military or Islamist: Egypt elected the Muslim Brotherhood, then the army under Sisi took it back in 2013, harder than before. Where the state collapsed outright, militias and patrons moved in: Libya split between rival governments backed by rival outsiders; Yemen's war became Saudi-versus-Houthi-and-Iran, the world's worst humanitarian crisis by UN count; Syria's Assad, backed by Russia from 2015 and Iran throughout, gassed and barrel-bombed through half a million dead and displaced half his country — remember from episode twenty how that chapter finally ended: the regime's sudden fall in December 2024, the frozen order liquefying. Tunisia, the one democracy standing, backslid a decade later. The counter-model that thrived: the Gulf monarchies — oil wealth buying quiescence, and a generational pivot visible in Saudi Vision 2030: diversify from oil, liberalize socially, centralize politically — reform without democracy, the region's live experiment. The through-line for the test: the region's contest is not ancient hatred; it is the unfinished question of what replaces the Ottoman-colonial state — nationalism, Islamism, monarchy, or democracy — with outside powers betting on answers. Recall block. The three wartime promises? Sykes-Picot to France and Britain; Balfour to Zionism; McMahon to the Arabs — incompatible, all partly kept. The Kurds were promised and denied where? Promised at Sèvres 1920, denied at Lausanne 1923. 1979's three revolutions? Iran's theocracy; Mecca and the Saudi hardening; Afghanistan and jihad's internationale. The 2003 lesson? Removing a regime without a state plan builds the vacuum — insurgency, Iran, ISIS. Why military-or-Islamist after the Spring? Repression razed everything else — only the army and the mosque survived organized. Next region: Europe and Russia — the EU machine and the empire that keeps returning. Thanks for listening.
