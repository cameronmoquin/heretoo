Transcript

Episode twenty-eight: English expression — the grammar rules the test actually tests, drilled by ear. Errors you can hear are errors you can fix at speed. Review first. The median survives what? Skew and outliers. Halving a margin of error costs? Four times the sample. Here we go — rule, then reps; call each sentence right or wrong in the pause, and fix it if wrong. Rule one: subject-verb agreement, where the test's trick is distance — words wedged between subject and verb to make your ear lose the thread. The subject is never inside a prepositional phrase. Listen: The report on visa backlogs and staffing shortages were submitted Friday. Wrong — the report was submitted; on backlogs and shortages is packaging. The ambassador, along with her senior staff, are attending. Wrong — along with is not and; the ambassador is attending. Each of the proposals have merit. Wrong — each is singular, always: each has merit. So are either, neither, everyone, and everybody. Neither the minister nor his deputies were available. Right — with neither-nor, the verb agrees with the nearer subject: deputies were. The committee has issued its report. Right — collective nouns acting as one unit take the singular. Rule two: pronouns — case, clarity, and agreement. Case first, and the ear-test is deletion: strip the other person and listen. The ambassador thanked my colleague and I. Wrong — thanked I? Thanked me. Between you and I is the same crime; prepositions take me, him, her. Whom should I say is calling? Wrong, elegantly — who is calling; who does the calling, so who it is. The swap test: he means who, him means whom. Clarity next: When the ambassador met the minister, he was late. Wrong — which he? An ambiguous pronoun has no single antecedent; name the latecomer. Agreement: Every officer must file their report by Friday. Formal test English still wants his or her, or better, the plural rewrite: all officers must file their reports. If the option set offers the rewrite, take it. Rule three: modifiers go next to what they modify, and the danglers are the comedy of the section. Walking through the embassy, the portraits impressed the visitors. Wrong — the portraits weren't walking; the visitors, walking through the embassy, were impressed. An opening phrase modifies the very next noun — make sure that noun is the doer. Having reviewed the cable, the decision was easy. Wrong — decisions don't review cables: having reviewed the cable, the officer decided easily. One-word modifiers slide too: The consul only approves visas on Tuesdays — as written, approving is all she does. If the meaning is the day, say approves visas only on Tuesdays. Put only just before the thing it limits. Rule four: parallelism — items in a series wear the same grammatical clothes. The job requires drafting cables, briefing visitors, and to manage the budget. Wrong — drafting, briefing, and managing. The program is affordable, effective, and it can be expanded. Wrong — affordable, effective, and expandable. And the paired conjunctions — not only, but also; either, or; both, and — must clamp matching parts: She is fluent not only in French but also in Arabic. Right — in French, in Arabic; the phrases match. Not only fluent in French but also Arabic would limp. Rule five: the comma splice and its cures. Two full sentences cannot hold hands with a comma. The talks collapsed, the delegations went home. Splice. Four cures: a period; a semicolon — the talks collapsed; the delegations went home; a conjunction — collapsed, so the delegations; or subordination — after the talks collapsed, the delegations went home. And know the impostors: however, therefore, and moreover are not conjunctions — the talks collapsed, however, the delegations stayed is still a splice; however needs a semicolon before it or a fresh sentence. Rule six, the lightning round — confusables, one cue each. Affect with an A acts; effect with an E is the end result. Fewer counts, less measures: fewer cables, less paperwork. Between two, among many. Its without an apostrophe owns; it's with one is it is. Their owns, there points, they're is they are. Principal is the main one — your pal; principle is the rule. Lie down yourself; lay down the file — lay needs an object. Who for people, that for things. Comprise: the whole comprises the parts — is comprised of is the trap. And imply-infer: speakers imply; listeners infer — the cable implied trouble; the desk officer inferred it. The gauntlet — five sentences, verdict and fix at speed. One: The data on migration flows suggests the policy is working. Formal English treats data as plural — the data suggest; if both appear as options, take suggest. Two: Hoping to reduce tensions, the treaty was signed by both nations. Dangler — hoping to reduce tensions, both nations signed the treaty. Three: The delegation comprises diplomats, economists, and interpreters. Right — the whole comprises its parts. Four: Whoever drafted this cable deserves credit. Right — whoever is the subject of drafted; the whole clause is the object. Five: The sanctions had less effects than expected. Two for one — fewer effects, and check that effect is the noun. That's the grammar layer. Next episode closes the season: ordering sentences, transitions, and concision — the architecture of paragraphs. Thanks for listening.
