The Diplomatic Toolkit and the Institutions

World Affairs · 7:40 · Episode 14

Transcript

Episode fourteen: the diplomatic toolkit and the institutions that hold the world together — or try. Review; out loud. The Rogers Act of 1924 created what? The professional Foreign Service. NATO reversed which founding advice? Washington's Farewell — no permanent alliances. The 1947 National Security Act created which three things? CIA, NSC, and the Defense Department. Three questions. One: what's the real difference between a treaty and an executive agreement, and why does it matter for durability? Two: what did Bretton Woods build, and what does each piece do today? Three: what tools sit on the shelf between a démarche and a war? Here we go. Start at home. The Secretary of State is the president's chief foreign policy officer, fourth in the line of succession — after the vice president, the Speaker, and the Senate's president pro tempore. Beneath the secretary: the deputy, the under secretaries, and the bureaus — regional ones like European and Eurasian Affairs, functional ones like Consular Affairs, which issues the passports and visas, and Diplomatic Security. Abroad, the embassy in each capital is led by an ambassador — the chief of mission, the president's personal representative — or, between ambassadors, a chargé d'affaires. Consulates in other cities handle visas and citizen services. The country team is the interagency in one room: State plus defense attachés, USAID, and the rest. Foreign Service Officers come in five career tracks, worth knowing since you're applying: political, economic, consular, management, and public diplomacy. And the rulebook of it all is the Vienna Convention on Diplomatic Relations, 1961: diplomatic immunity, the inviolability of embassies, and persona non grata — the host country's right to expel any diplomat without explanation, the standard move in spy scandals. One precision the test rewards: an embassy is not technically the sender's sovereign territory; it is inviolable, which means the host may not enter without permission — a different thing. Question one: treaties versus executive agreements. Constitutionally, a treaty needs two-thirds of the Senate; an executive agreement needs only the president's signature. In volume, executive agreements dominate modern practice by more than ten to one. The trade-off is durability: a treaty is law, hard to make and hard to unmake; an executive agreement lives and dies with presidential will. The Iran nuclear deal was an executive agreement — one signature made it in 2015, one withdrawal unmade it in 2018. The Paris climate accord: same story, left and rejoined by pen stroke. NATO, a real treaty, has outlived fourteen presidents. When a test question asks why a president chose an executive agreement, the answer is speed and the Senate; when it asks the cost, the answer is impermanence. Midpoint check. Persona non grata means what, and who declares it? The host state expels a diplomat, no reason required — the receiving country's power, not the sender's. Question two: the postwar architecture, built in two rooms. Room one, Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, 1944, the economic order: the International Monetary Fund, lender to countries in balance-of-payments crisis, attaching conditions to its rescues; and the World Bank, development lender for infrastructure and poverty reduction. The trade leg came third: GATT, 1947, rounds of tariff cuts, becoming the World Trade Organization in 1995 with binding dispute settlement. Keep the trio straight by their patients: IMF treats currency crises, the World Bank treats poverty, the WTO referees trade fights. Room two, San Francisco, 1945: the United Nations. The Security Council holds the real power — fifteen members, ten rotating, five permanent with vetoes: the United States, United Kingdom, France, Russia, China. Chapter seven resolutions authorize sanctions and force; one veto stops everything, which is both the design flaw and the design — the great powers stay inside the tent because the tent can't be used against them. The General Assembly: every state, one vote, resolutions without binding force. The International Court of Justice at The Hague settles disputes between consenting states. Separate and confused with it constantly: the International Criminal Court, also at The Hague, prosecuting individuals for genocide, war crimes, and crimes against humanity — and the United States is not a party to it. ICJ, states; ICC, persons; America, in the first, out of the second. Question three: the escalation shelf, from mildest to strongest. A démarche — a formal diplomatic protest, the Foreign Service's basic unit of displeasure. Recalling an ambassador for consultations — a signal, cheap and reversible. Public condemnation and coalition statements. Foreign aid, given, conditioned, or withheld — USAID's development assistance and security assistance both double as leverage. Sanctions, the modern workhorse: targeted ones freeze individuals' assets and travel; sectoral ones hit banking, energy, or arms; secondary sanctions punish third parties who trade with the target — the tool that makes American sanctions global, since access to the dollar system is the hostage. Expulsions and embassy closures. Breaking diplomatic relations entirely. Covert action, presidentially authorized with congressional notification. Blockade — legally an act of war. Then force, with the War Powers Resolution's clock ticking from the first strike. The craft is matching the rung to the offense — and the test's favorite fact on the shelf: sanctions work best multilaterally, because unilateral sanctions reroute trade rather than stop it. Recall block. Treaty versus executive agreement, the trade-off in one line? Two-thirds of the Senate buys durability; a signature buys speed and impermanence. The Bretton Woods trio and their patients? IMF, currency crises; World Bank, development; WTO — born GATT — trade disputes. The five permanent Security Council members? US, UK, France, Russia, China. ICJ versus ICC? ICJ tries disputes between states; ICC tries individuals; the US accepts only the first. Secondary sanctions hit whom? Third parties trading with the target — dollar access as leverage. Next: we leave the present and build the world history spine — everything before 1789 that the test expects you to place. Thanks for listening.
