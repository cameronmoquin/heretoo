Logic Drill 3, Strengthen and Weaken

Transcript

Logic drill three: strengthen and weaken. The recap, out loud. Composition is — whole from parts. Appeal to ignorance is — absence of proof as proof of absence. Affirming the consequent is — if A then B, B, therefore A. Today's drill is the money skill: most strengthen-weaken questions are causal arguments in disguise, and causal arguments live or die on one thing — alternative explanations. Weaken by supplying one; strengthen by killing one. Same format: argument twice, question, silence, answer out loud. Argument one. Countries that join the trade pact grow faster than countries that don't. Therefore joining the pact causes growth. Again: pact members grow faster than non-members; so joining causes growth. What one fact would most weaken this? Out loud. Anything showing selection: for instance, that the pact only admits countries already growing fast — stable institutions, open markets. Then membership doesn't cause growth; growth causes membership, or a third thing causes both. Observational comparisons — members versus non-members, trainees versus non-trainees, study-abroad students versus homebodies — always hide this question: who selected in? If the groups differed before the treatment, the comparison proves nothing. Selection effect: it was the weakener in drill one's promotion argument, and it will be the weakener on your test. Argument two. After the city installed streetlights, night crime fell fifteen percent. The streetlights reduced crime. Again: streetlights installed, night crime down fifteen percent, so the lights did it. What would most strengthen this? A control: a similar neighborhood, no new lights, where night crime stayed flat or rose over the same period. The comparison rules out the biggest rival story — that crime was falling everywhere anyway. Strengthening a causal claim is subtraction: remove rival explanations one by one. Also strengthening: the drop concentrated exactly where the lights went in, or the drop tracking the installation timeline block by block. Dose matching effect is causation's fingerprint. Argument three. Ministers who attended the summit now support the agreement. The summit clearly persuaded them. Again: attendees now support the agreement; the summit persuaded them. Weaken it. Show they supported it before walking in — attendance was voluntary, and supporters chose to come. No before-and-after, no causation: a snapshot after the fact cannot distinguish persuasion from self-selection. The general weapon: demand the baseline. It's the same move as the vaccine question from the app — sixty percent coverage sounds like failure until you learn it was twenty before the campaign. Numbers without baselines are stories without beginnings. Argument four. Our embassy's language program must be effective: ninety percent of participants pass the fluency exam. Again: the program must work; ninety percent of participants pass. Weaken it. Two clean options. The baseline again: what share would pass without the program? If entrants are screened for existing fluency, ninety percent proves screening, not teaching. Or attack the measure: if the exam is easy, passing means little. Notice what does not weaken it: the program is expensive; some students disliked it; other programs also work. Irrelevant criticism is the test's favorite wrong answer — it sounds negative but never touches the causal link. Before choosing a weakener, ask: does this fact make the conclusion less likely, or does it just frown at it? Argument five. Since the sanctions began, the regime's exports have fallen forty percent. The sanctions are working. Again: exports down forty percent since sanctions began; sanctions are working. Weaken. Alternative cause: a global recession, a crop failure, a currency collapse — anything that would cut exports with or without sanctions. Or attack the goal: if working means changing the regime's behavior, falling exports may be the wrong measure entirely — exports can fall while the behavior continues. That's the two-pronged attack on any policy-success claim: another cause for the data, or a gap between the data and the goal. Argument six, the full anatomy, you do everything. A think tank reports: nations with higher educational spending have lower unemployment. The legislature should therefore raise educational spending to cut unemployment. Once more: higher education spending correlates with lower unemployment; so raise spending to cut unemployment. First — what kind of evidence is this? A correlation across nations: no experiment, no baseline, no time order established. Second — name two rival explanations. Reverse causation: rich, low-unemployment economies can afford more schooling. Or a common cause: strong economies produce both budgets and jobs. Third — what single piece of evidence would most strengthen it? Something with time order and a control: nations that raised spending subsequently saw unemployment fall, compared with similar nations that didn't. You just performed the entire checklist: classify the evidence, generate rivals, name the cure. That is the skill, whole. The drill's four weapons, out loud. Against member-versus-nonmember comparisons? Selection effect. Against after-therefore-because? Alternative cause, or no baseline. To strengthen causation? Controls, and dose matching effect. The favorite wrong answer? Irrelevant criticism — negative in tone, untouched causal link. Next: the foreign policy block begins — every doctrine from Washington's Farewell to today, as one chain. Thanks for listening.
