OMST episode three: situational judgment drills. Review first; answer out loud. How many selections does each scenario ask for? Two. The best answer choice and the worst answer choice from the options presented, as State's specialist assessment guide puts it. Which do you pick first, and why? The worst, because a clearly wrong answer is easier to recognise than a best answer competing with a good one. Across forty keyed items, which defect was the worst answer most often? Working around the problem instead of acting on it: seventeen of forty. Breaking a rule was rarest, four. Three questions for this episode. One: which of the four questions does most of the work on the worst pick? Two: once the worst pick is gone, what separates two options that are both defensible? Three: how much Department procedure do you need for any of this? Take the third now. None. State's own practice instructions say knowledge of the Department's policies, procedures, or organizational culture is not required. The scenes are set in an embassy; they are ordinary problems between working adults. The four questions, once more, fast. Does the option break a rule. Does it act on the problem, or work around it. Does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it. Is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Twelve scenarios. I read the scene, then go quiet long enough for both picks: say the worst out loud, then the best. Three of these turn on a rule, a heavier concentration than the keyed items run, so do not leave here hunting rule-breakers. One. You manage the Ambassador's calendar. A long-standing meeting with the Foreign Minister now collides with an urgent country team meeting the DCM has just called. Worst, then best. Worst: leave both on the calendar and let it resolve itself at the door. It works around the problem instead of acting. Best: tell the Ambassador now, with two workable options and your recommendation. She is the nearest person who can fix it, because the time is hers. Surface the conflict, do the staff work, let the principal choose. Two. A colleague under deadline pressure asks you to send classified content over the unclassified system, saying it is only mildly sensitive and they are cleared anyway. Worst: send it. Breaks a rule, and the first question ends the argument before the deadline or the clearance gets a hearing. Best: decline, say why, and offer the classified channel in the same breath. Declining and stopping there holds the rule and still works around the problem: the deadline is untouched. Three. You find a document marked SECRET unattended on a shared printer. Worst: turn it face down and leave it. That treats the appearance, not the exposure, which is working around the problem in its purest form. Best: take control of it at once, secure it, and notify the owner and the security office. Carrying it around to find the owner extends the exposure; naming whoever you assume printed it reports a suspicion as a fact. Four. You realize you emailed a spreadsheet of personally identifiable information to the wrong distribution list. The recall appears to have worked. Worst: treat the recall as the fix and say nothing. You cannot verify that it worked, and silence works around it. Best: report it through the required channel immediately and follow the guidance you get. Emailing the recipients yourself acts, but it is the wrong person doing it and widens the exposure. Five. A colleague has missed deadlines three times running, it is delaying work you owe your supervisor, and you have said nothing to them. Worst: tell your supervisor the colleague is unreliable. You have three dates and no reason, so that is a judgment about a person built on what you suspect. Best: raise it with them directly, name the downstream effect, and agree a deadline. They are the nearest person who can fix it. Saying nothing and absorbing the delay is the other wrong shape. Six. A congressional delegation arriving tomorrow has just added two meetings and moved its departure earlier. Worst: tell the delegation the schedule is fixed. Nothing about the problem moves. Best: work out immediately what is still feasible, take the options and the tradeoffs to the control officer, and rebook what is approved. Rebooking everything first and reporting afterward acts, but the control officer owns the visit. Assess, present, then act on the approval. Midpoint check. Four of the first six worst picks shared one defect. Which? The option that worked around the problem instead of acting on it. When two options both act and both hold the rule, what separates the best from the merely defensible? The one that also keeps the working relationship intact. Seven. A vendor who does regular business with the embassy offers you an expensive gift after a successful event. Worst: accept it and say nothing. Breaks a rule. Best: decline it and report the offer as the rules require. The tempting detail is the timing, after the work is done. The vendor will be back. Accepting it to share with the office is the same rule broken with better manners. Eight. Your supervisor is on a flight and unreachable. A routine decision inside your delegated authority has to be made today. Worst: hold it until they land. Delegated authority exists to be used, and waiting is working around the problem with a good excuse. Best: make it, document what you did and why, and brief your supervisor as soon as they are reachable. Hunting up another supervisor to sign it fails the third question: the nearest person who can fix this is you. Documentation separates initiative from freelancing. Nine. You are new at post. A locally employed colleague with fifteen years there suggests handling a routine submission differently from how you were trained at your last post. Worst: quietly check with an American colleague before believing them. You have no reason to doubt the person in front of you: the fourth question failing, and the third with it. Best: ask them to explain the reasoning, and adopt the local practice if it is sound and compliant. Defaulting to your old post's habits is the working-around version. Ten. You spot a factual error in a document your supervisor has already approved and sent up for clearance. Worst: correct the file copy and leave the sent version travelling. The record you fixed is not the record in motion. Best: tell your supervisor at once, privately, so the correction is made before the document goes further. Sending your own correction up the clearance chain goes past the nearest person who can fix it. Eleven. An old friend asks whether you can check on, or speed up, their pending visa application. Worst: ask a consular colleague to move it along for you. Breaks a rule. Best: decline to intervene and refer them to the published process and the public inquiry channels. The trap is graduated: just checking the status, just hand-delivering the documents, because a small favour feels like no favour. Any action in a specific case by someone with insider access is improper. Twelve, the hardest shape here. Two officers, neither your direct supervisor, each tell you their task is your top priority today, and you cannot do both. Worst: pick one silently and let the other find out when the product does not arrive. Someone is planning around work that is not coming, and that surprise is the actual failure. Best: tell each officer about the other's request and ask your supervisor to set the priority; your supervisor owns your time. When you cannot satisfy two commitments, the unforgivable act is letting someone find out late. Recall block. How many picks per scenario, and which one first? Two. The worst first. Name the four questions. Does it break a rule. Does it act on the problem, or work around it. Does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it. Is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Which defect is the worst answer most often, and which least? Working around the problem, seventeen of forty. Breaking a rule, four. How much Department procedure does this section require? None. Next episode: reading comprehension, and the mechanical method for beating a passage under a clock. Thanks for listening.
