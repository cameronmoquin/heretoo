Transcript

OMST episode three: situational judgment drills. Twelve scenarios. I read the scene, then go quiet. Say your answer out loud before I give mine, and say which step of the method decided it. The method, once more, fast: who owns the decision; what category; cut the rule-breakers; cut the passive; act within authority and inform; sooner beats later. One. You manage the Ambassador's calendar. A long-standing meeting with the Foreign Minister now collides with an urgent country team meeting the DCM has just called. What do you do? Inform the Ambassador immediately, with two workable options and your recommendation. Step one decides it: the decision is the Ambassador's, not yours. Cancelling either meeting yourself is the bulldozer; leaving both on the calendar so it resolves itself at the door is the ostrich. Note the shape of the keyed answer, because it recurs all day: surface the conflict, do the staff work, let the principal choose. Two. A colleague under deadline pressure asks you to send classified content over the unclassified email system, saying it is only mildly sensitive and they are cleared anyway. What do you do? Decline, say why, and immediately offer to send it through the classified channel. Step two flagged the category as security, and step three cut every option that moves the material. The deadline is real, the colleague is cleared, and neither fact touches the rule. Notice the second half of the answer: you still solve their problem, by the compliant route. Declining without offering the path is a weaker answer. Three. You find a document marked SECRET sitting unattended on a shared printer. What do you do? Take control of it at once, secure it, and notify the owner and the security office. It is a security incident from the moment it is unattended. Turning it face down is the deflector, solving the appearance rather than the problem. Carrying it to the next staff meeting to find the owner extends the exposure and adds a second violation. Four. You realize you have emailed a spreadsheet containing personally identifiable information to the wrong distribution list. The recall appears to have worked. What do you do? Report it immediately through the required channel and follow the guidance you are given. The apparent success of the recall changes nothing; a spillage is reportable on its own terms. This is the self-report rule, and it is one of the three hard rules from episode two: mistakes are reported early, by you, with an assessment of the damage. Five. A colleague has missed deadlines three times running, and it is delaying work you owe your supervisor. You have not yet said anything to them. What is the best first step? Raise it with the colleague directly, explain the downstream effect, and agree a workable deadline. Going to your supervisor first is the escalator. And note the stem said first step, which is exactly the qualifier from last episode: with a different stem, asking for the best course of action across the whole problem, escalating after a direct conversation fails would be in play. Read the stem twice. Six. A congressional delegation arriving tomorrow has just added two meetings and moved its departure earlier. What do you do? Assess immediately what is still feasible, take the options and the tradeoffs to the control officer, and start rebooking what is approved. The control officer owns the visit. Rebooking everything first and reporting afterward is the bulldozer; telling the delegation the schedule is fixed is the ostrich wearing a uniform. The keyed answer is again assess, present, then act on the approval. Seven. A vendor who does regular business with the embassy offers you an expensive gift after a successful event. What do you do? Decline it and report the offer as the ethics rules require. Category: ethics, so the ordinary virtue of graciousness is inverted. The tempting detail is the timing, after the work is done, which feels like it removes the influence. It does not; the vendor will be back. Accepting and sharing it with the office is the people-pleaser and does not cure anything. Eight. Your supervisor is on a flight and unreachable. A routine decision that falls within your delegated authority has to be made today. What do you do? Make it, document what you did and why, and brief your supervisor as soon as they are reachable. Delegated authority exists to be used, so waiting until they land is the ostrich. The two clauses again: act, and inform. The documentation is what separates initiative from freelancing, and it is the part candidates most often leave out. Nine. You are new at post. A locally employed colleague with fifteen years there suggests handling a routine submission differently from how you were trained at your last post. What do you do? Ask them to explain the reasoning, and adopt the local practice if it is sound and compliant. Long-serving local staff are the institutional memory of the building. Defaulting to your previous post's habits discards the most useful expertise available to you, and checking with an American colleague before believing them is its own quiet insult. Ten. You spot a factual error in a document your supervisor has already approved and sent up for clearance. What do you do? Tell your supervisor at once, privately, so the correction can be made before the document goes further. Timing, step six, decides this: the document is moving. Correcting it quietly in the file copy leaves the wrong version traveling upward, and raising it in the next staff meeting is both too slow and needlessly public. Eleven. An old friend contacts you to ask whether you can check on, or speed up, their pending visa application. What do you do? Decline to intervene and refer them to the published process and the public inquiry channels. Ethics again. The trap here is graduated: the test will offer you a smaller version, just checking the status, or just hand-delivering their documents, because a small favor feels like no favor. Any action by someone with insider access is improper in a specific case. There is no small version. Twelve, the hardest shape on the test. Two officers, neither your direct supervisor, each tell you their task is your top priority today, and you cannot do both. What do you do? Tell each officer about the other's request and ask your supervisor to set the priority. Step one: the decision about your priorities belongs to the person who owns your time. Silently picking by rank, or by which task is shorter, leaves the other officer expecting a product that is not coming, and that surprise is the actual failure. The general principle: when you cannot satisfy two commitments, the unforgivable act is letting someone find out late. Recall block. What is the two-clause shape of most keyed answers? Act within your authority, and inform the right person. What does the qualifier first license? A clarifying or assessing step that would otherwise read as passive. In a security or ethics scenario, which ordinary virtues become wrong answers? Helpfulness, flexibility, and discretion on a colleague's behalf. What is the unforgivable act when you cannot meet a commitment? Letting the other person find out late.
