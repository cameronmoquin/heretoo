Transcript

Episode twenty-nine, the season finale: paragraph architecture — ordering sentences, transitions, and concision, the skills behind every organize-this-passage question. Review; out loud. The cure list for a comma splice? Period, semicolon, conjunction, or subordination. An opening modifier attaches to? The very next noun — which must be the doer. Fewer or less paperwork? Less — paperwork measures; cables count. Three questions. One: how do you find the first sentence of a scrambled paragraph without reading for meaning? Two: what do transition words fix about position? Three: what does concision actually delete? Here we go. Question one: anchors, not comprehension. The first sentence of a paragraph can stand alone — no pronoun waiting for a name, no this or these reaching backward, no however contradicting something unsaid, no then following nothing. Scrambled-sentence items are solved by disqualification: cross out every sentence that leans on an antecedent, and what remains standing is your opener. Drill it. Three sentences; pick the opener. A: These reforms, however, faced immediate resistance. B: The ministry announced a package of economic reforms in March. C: It collapsed within a year. B — A leans on these and however; C leans on it. B stands alone. Then chain the rest by antecedents: these reforms needs reforms named first, so A follows B; it needs a singular thing — the package — so C closes. B, A, C. Announce, complicate, resolve — most test paragraphs are that story. Question two: transitions as GPS coordinates — each one fixes a sentence relative to its neighbors, which is why they're free points. For example must follow a general claim — it points backward at something to exemplify. As a result follows a cause. However, nevertheless, even so follow the thing they push against — contrast lives after its target, often last. In fact follows a claim it intensifies. Meanwhile runs parallel in time. Ordinals are the freest points of all: first, second, then, finally build the skeleton by themselves. Drill: a sentence begins with by contrast — what must precede it? A sentence making the opposite case — by contrast cannot open a paragraph cold. A sentence begins for instance — its predecessor is? A general statement it illustrates. And the odd-one-out question — which sentence doesn't belong — has the same mechanical answer: the intruder changes subject, not tone; four sentences about visa policy and one about embassy parking, however gracefully written, and the parking loses. Midpoint check. Why does however almost never start a well-built paragraph? Because it contradicts something — and at paragraph-start, nothing's been said to contradict; its home is after the claim it opposes. Question three: concision — the test's editing questions reward the shortest version that keeps the meaning, and the flab comes in named varieties. Redundant pairs: each and every, first and foremost, full and complete, true facts, final outcome, past history, advance planning, close proximity — every one of them is a single word wearing two coats. Say it tighter: at this point in time? Now. Due to the fact that? Because. In the event that? If. Has the ability to? Can. Conduct an investigation into? Investigate — and that's the nominalization pattern: verbs smothered into nouns; make a decision, reach a conclusion, provide assistance — decide, conclude, help. The passive voice earns its keep only when the doer is unknown or beside the point; otherwise the active is shorter and clearer. The proposal was rejected by the committee? The committee rejected the proposal. But the ambassador was recalled — passive, and fine, if who recalled her is not the news. Passive isn't wrong; unmotivated passive is. Now the full drill — a scrambled paragraph, four sentences; assemble it out loud. A: Finally, the agreement was initialed at dawn. B: Negotiations opened with both sides far apart on tariffs. C: However, a compromise on agricultural goods broke the deadlock. D: For three days, neither delegation moved. Again: A, finally, initialed at dawn. B, negotiations opened far apart. C, however, a compromise broke the deadlock. D, for three days neither moved. Order them. B, D, C, A. B stands alone and opens. D's neither delegation needs both sides named — it follows. C's however pushes against the deadlock D built. A's finally closes. Opener by independence, then antecedents, then contrast, then the ordinal. Every tool in one paragraph. Recall block, and a season close. The opener test? Stands alone — no pronouns, no this, no however, no then. However lives where? After the thing it contradicts. The odd sentence out changes? Subject, not tone. Nominalization is? A verb buried in a noun — dig it out: decide, conclude, help. And that's the spine — twenty-nine episodes, every section of the test: history as cause and effect, the government machine, the doctrines and the world board, money, maps, numbers, logic, and language. From here, relisten where the pauses beat you — the episode that makes you hesitate is the one to replay — and pair the listening with the practice app, because retrieval on the screen is what the retrieval in the car was building toward. Deeper dives are coming in season two. Good luck with the test — you've been studying the whole drive. Thanks for listening.
