Episode eleven: the amendments in runs, and the liberties they carry. Review; out loud. What doctrinal move applied the Bill of Rights to the states? Incorporation, through the Fourteenth Amendment's due process clause. Marshall Court's project in two words? Federal power. Dobbs overruled — Roe. Three questions. One: why do the amendments cluster in bursts instead of arriving steadily? Two: which pairs of amendments does the test love to confuse, and how do you keep them straight? Three: what does each First Amendment clause actually protect, and where are the edges? Here we go. The master insight: amendments come in waves, and each wave has one author — a crisis. Learn the waves, and the numbers file themselves. Wave one, 1791: the Bill of Rights, one through ten, the price of ratification. Wave two, the early fixes: eleven, 1798, state sovereign immunity from suits, reversing a Court decision; twelve, 1804, separate ballots for president and vice president, patching the tie of 1800 that nearly made Aaron Burr president. Wave three, Reconstruction, 1865 to 1870: thirteen, fourteen, fifteen — abolition, citizenship and equal protection, the vote regardless of race. One war, three amendments. Wave four, the Progressive burst, 1913 to 1920: sixteen, income tax; seventeen, direct election of senators; eighteen, Prohibition; nineteen, women's suffrage. One movement, four amendments. Wave five, mid-century housekeeping: twenty, 1933, the lame-duck fix, moving inauguration to January; twenty-one, 1933, repeal of Prohibition — the only amendment ratified by state conventions; twenty-two, 1951, the two-term limit, Roosevelt's four terms answered. Wave six, the rights era: twenty-three, 1961, electors for D.C.; twenty-four, 1964, no poll tax in federal elections; twenty-five, 1967, presidential disability and succession, written after Kennedy's assassination; twenty-six, 1971, vote at eighteen — old enough to be drafted, old enough to vote. And the straggler: twenty-seven, congressional pay raises wait for an election, proposed 1789, ratified 1992. So question one's answer: the Constitution amends itself the way geology moves — long stillness, then an earthquake named Reconstruction, Progressivism, or the sixties. Question two, the trap pairs, and here is the disambiguation kit. Sixteen versus seventeen, both 1913: sixteen taxes income, seventeen elects senators — tax before senate, numerically and alphabetically. Eighteen versus twenty-one: Prohibition on, Prohibition off — the bar opens at twenty-one. Twenty-four versus twenty-six: poll tax versus voting age — twenty-four kills the tax, twenty-six lowers the age, and the numbers rhyme with their jobs: twenty-four dollars, twenty-six candles. Fourteen versus fifteen: citizenship versus the ballot — you must be a citizen before you vote, fourteen before fifteen. And twelve versus twenty-five, the two vice-presidential patches: twelve gets him elected properly, twenty-five replaces him properly. Midpoint check. The Twenty-first was ratified by state conventions instead of legislatures — why? Congress suspected dry legislatures would block repeal, so it routed around them to convention delegates — proof the framers' alternate ratification track had a purpose. Question three: the First Amendment, clause by clause, because the test asks edges, not slogans. Speech: strongly protected, including hateful and offensive speech; the narrow exceptions are incitement to imminent lawless action — Brandenburg, 1969, replacing Schenck's looser clear-and-present-danger test — true threats, defamation with the required fault, and obscenity. Symbolic conduct counts: flag burning is protected, Texas versus Johnson, 1989. Press: no prior restraint absent extraordinary harm — Near, 1931, and the Pentagon Papers; and Sullivan's actual-malice rule shields criticism of public officials. Religion, two clauses in tension: establishment — no official church, no government endorsement — and free exercise — belief absolutely protected, conduct mostly so. Assembly and petition round it out. The remaining Bill of Rights in one breath each: second, arms — an individual right after Heller; third, no quartering, the amendment that never gets litigated; fourth, no unreasonable searches, warrants on probable cause — Mapp's exclusionary rule enforces it; fifth, the criminal-procedure bundle — grand jury, double jeopardy, self-incrimination, due process, takings with just compensation; sixth, the trial bundle — speedy, public, jury, confrontation, counsel; seventh, civil juries; eighth, no cruel and unusual punishment, no excessive bail; ninth, unenumerated rights retained by the people; tenth, reserved powers to the states — federalism's bookend. Fifth versus sixth is a test favorite: fifth is what the government cannot do to you; sixth is what your trial must give you. One more distinction that decides questions: civil liberties versus civil rights. Liberties are freedoms from government — speech, religion, searches. Rights are guarantees of equal treatment — voting, public accommodations, nondiscrimination, enforced by the Fourteenth and Fifteenth Amendments and statutes like the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965. A question about school prayer is liberties; a question about lunch counters is rights. Recall block. The five waves, one word each — 1791? Ratification. 1865 to 70? Reconstruction. 1913 to 20? Progressivism. 1933 to 51? Housekeeping — lame duck, repeal, term limit. The sixties run? Rights — D.C., poll tax, succession, age eighteen. Sixteen and seventeen, which is which? Sixteen income tax, seventeen direct election of senators. Brandenburg's speech test? Incitement to imminent lawless action — mere advocacy is protected. Fifth versus sixth? Fifth restrains the government; sixth equips your trial. Next: a logic drill on strengthening and weakening arguments. Then we go abroad for the foreign policy block. Thanks for listening.
