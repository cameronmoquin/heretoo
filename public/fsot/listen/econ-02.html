The Machinery, Micro and Macro

Economics · 8:02 · Episode 22

Open the full player

Transcript

Episode twenty-two: the machinery — the micro and macro concepts the test actually scores. Review; out loud. Smith, Ricardo, Keynes, Friedman — each one's headline? Invisible hand; comparative advantage; aggregate demand; inflation is monetary. The paradox of thrift? Simultaneous saving shrinks everyone's income. Three questions. One: what moves a curve versus what moves along it — the distinction that decides most micro questions? Two: what exactly is GDP, and what does it deliberately leave out? Three: what are the Fed's actual tools, and which one is the workhorse? Here we go. Micro first. Demand slopes down — cheaper means more bought; supply slopes up — pricier means more offered; they cross at equilibrium. Question one is the trap the test sets constantly: a price change moves you along a curve; anything else that changes behavior shifts the whole curve. Coffee gets cheaper, you buy more — movement along. Your income rises, a study says coffee cures ailments, tea's price triples — the demand curve itself shifts right. Drought kills the harvest, wages of pickers rise — supply shifts left, and price rises without demand moving at all. The stem's first job: is this a price change, or a conditions change? Elasticity measures sensitivity: elastic demand stretches — luxuries, things with substitutes; inelastic barely budges — insulin, gasoline, salt. The revenue rule worth money on the test: raising price raises revenue only when demand is inelastic; tax something inelastic and the buyer eats the tax. Price controls: a ceiling below equilibrium — rent control — creates shortage; a floor above it — minimum wage in the textbook model — creates surplus; both suppress the price signal that would clear the market. Externalities: costs or benefits dumped on bystanders — pollution negative, vaccination positive; the standard fixes are taxes on the harm — Pigouvian — subsidies on the benefit, or tradable permits, which is cap and trade. Public goods — defense, lighthouses — are non-excludable and non-rival, so markets underprovide them; that free-rider logic is also NATO burden-sharing, one concept in two costumes. Question two: GDP — the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country in a year. Each word earns its place. Final: intermediate goods don't double-count — the steel inside the car counts once, in the car. Within a country: that's domestic — American GDP includes the Toyota plant in Kentucky, excludes the Ford plant in Germany; GNP tracks ownership instead, and the gap matters mainly for remittance economies. What it leaves out, by design: unpaid household work, the informal economy, leisure, environmental damage, and distribution — a country can grow while its median household doesn't. The expenditure formula: consumption plus investment plus government purchases plus net exports — C plus I plus G plus X minus M. Watch investment: in GDP it means business spending on plant, equipment, and inventory, not buying stocks. Real versus nominal: real strips out inflation; per capita divides by population — and from the fast-math lesson, per capita growth is roughly GDP growth minus population growth. Alternatives the test names: the Human Development Index — income plus health plus education, Sen's fingerprints — and purchasing power parity, which reprices output at common prices, boosting poor economies' measured size. Midpoint check. A tariff on imported steel: what happens to the domestic steel supply-and-demand picture? Domestic producers gain — they sell more at higher prices; steel-using industries and consumers pay more; the tariff is a tax whose burden spreads down the chain. Protection is a transfer, not a free lunch. The macro dashboard, three gauges. Unemployment: the share of the labor force seeking work and not finding it — and the trap is the denominator: discouraged workers who quit looking leave the labor force, so unemployment can fall while the situation worsens; check the participation rate. Three flavors: frictional — between jobs, healthy; structural — skills or geography mismatched, the painful kind; cyclical — the slump kind that policy targets. Inflation: the general price level rising, measured by the Consumer Price Index; deflation is falling prices — and it's feared more than mild inflation, because falling prices make debts heavier and reward waiting, which shrinks spending — thirties logic. Hyperinflation is the printing-press disease: Weimar, Zimbabwe, Venezuela. The real-versus-nominal reflex from the math lesson: real interest equals nominal minus inflation; a ten percent raise under twelve percent inflation is a pay cut. Recession: conventionally two consecutive quarters of shrinking real GDP. Question three: the Federal Reserve — created 1913, remember your Progressive amendments cluster. Dual mandate: price stability and maximum employment. Structure: seven governors in Washington, twelve regional banks, and the Federal Open Market Committee setting policy. The tools. The workhorse: setting the federal funds rate — the overnight interbank rate — historically through open market operations, buying and selling Treasuries to move reserves; rates ripple outward into mortgages, business loans, everything. The rest of the kit: the discount rate for direct lending to banks, reserve requirements, interest paid on reserves, and, since 2008, quantitative easing — large-scale asset purchases to push down long rates when the short rate hits zero. The transmission: raise rates, borrowing slows, demand cools, inflation eases — at the price of jobs; cut rates, the reverse. That's the loop you watched in 2008, 2020, and the inflation fight after. Fiscal policy is the other lever — Congress taxing and spending, Keynes's channel — slower to move, harder to reverse. And the Fed's independence exists precisely so that money isn't managed on election calendars; the test likes that rationale. Recall block. Movement along versus shift — the one-question test? Did the good's own price change, or did conditions change? GDP's four letters? C plus I plus G plus net exports. Deflation is feared because? Debts heavier, buyers wait, spending spirals down. The Fed's dual mandate and workhorse tool? Stable prices and maximum employment; the federal funds rate via open market operations. Tax something inelastic and who pays? The buyer — demand barely moves. Next: trade, currencies, and the trilemma — economics crossing borders. Thanks for listening.
