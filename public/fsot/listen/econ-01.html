The Economic Thought Chain

Economics · 7:50 · Episode 21

Open the full player

Transcript

Episode twenty-one: the economic thought chain — every economist the test names, as one running argument. Review; out loud. Pyongyang's bomb is rational because? Regime insurance — the Iraq-Libya lesson. Resolution 242's formula? Land for peace. Who holds Western Sahara? Morocco. Three questions. One: what was mercantilism, and what single book demolished it? Two: what problem did Keynes solve that classical economics couldn't? Three: who answered Keynes, and what did the argument settle into? The frame: economics advances as a chain of rebuttals — each thinker fixes the last one's failure. Walk the chain and the names order themselves. Link one: mercantilism, the operating system of Europe from the fifteen hundreds to the seventeen hundreds. Wealth is treasure; the world's stock of it is fixed; so export more than you import, hoard the gold, and hold colonies as captive customers and raw-material farms. Navigation Acts, chartered monopolies, tariff walls — and notice, from your American history, that this is exactly the system the colonies revolted inside. Link two is the demolition, and question one's answer: Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations, 1776. Wealth is not gold; it is what a nation can produce. The division of labor multiplies production; self-interest, disciplined by competition, serves the public — the invisible hand; and trade is not zero-sum: both sides gain, or they wouldn't trade. Free markets over managed hoards. The founding text of economics, published the year America declared independence — the pairing you already know. The classical school builds on Smith. David Ricardo, 1817, adds the discipline's most counterintuitive theorem: comparative advantage. Even a country worse at producing everything gains by specializing where its disadvantage is smallest and trading — it's opportunity cost, not absolute skill, that matters. This is the intellectual foundation under every free trade agreement, the WTO, and half the economics questions on your test. Thomas Malthus, 1798, contributes the gloom: population grows geometrically, food arithmetically, so misery is the equilibrium — wrong for two centuries because he missed technology, but the reason economics is the dismal science. John Stuart Mill synthesizes classical liberalism at mid-century. Then the great dissent: Karl Marx — the Communist Manifesto, 1848, Das Kapital, 1867. Capitalism's engine is the extraction of surplus value from labor; its contradictions produce crises and, he predicts, revolution. As prophecy it failed in the industrial West and triumphed as ideology elsewhere — you've met the consequences in every Cold War episode. And Alfred Marshall, 1890, gives the field its toolbox: supply and demand curves crossing at equilibrium, elasticity, marginal analysis — the diagrams in every textbook since. Midpoint check. Comparative advantage says trade pays even when — what? Even when one country is worse at producing everything — specialization by smallest disadvantage, opportunity cost, both sides gain. Question two: the Depression breaks the classical machine. Classical theory said markets self-correct — wages and prices fall, full employment returns. From 1929 to 1933 output collapsed by a quarter, unemployment hit twenty-five percent, and no correction came. John Maynard Keynes — the same Keynes who walked out of Versailles predicting ruin — publishes The General Theory in 1936: economies can stick in underemployment because the problem is insufficient aggregate demand; frightened households and firms all save at once, and one person's spending is another's income, so thrift compounds the fall — the paradox of thrift. The cure: when private demand fails, government spends — deficits in slumps, the multiplier amplifying each dollar. You watched the theory in action in episode four: the New Deal groping toward it, the war proving it. Bretton Woods, 1944 — the IMF and World Bank from episode fourteen — is Keynes's institutional handiwork too; he led the British delegation. Question three: the answers to Keynes. Friedrich Hayek, The Road to Serfdom, 1944: central planning, however well-meant, concentrates power and erodes liberty — the market is an information system no planner can replicate. Milton Friedman, from the 1960s: monetarism — inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon; the Depression's true cause was the Fed letting the money supply collapse; and the Keynesian trade-off between inflation and unemployment breaks down once people expect inflation. The 1970s proved him right on schedule: stagflation — high inflation and high unemployment together, which Keynesian theory said couldn't happen — remember Carter's inheritance. The counter-revolution crested with Reagan and Thatcher, plus supply-side economics: cut marginal tax rates to spur production, the Laffer curve's claim that rates can be so high that cutting them raises revenue. The argument settled into today's synthesis: markets allocate, central banks manage inflation and slumps with Friedman's lessons, governments deploy Keynes in crises — 2008 and 2020 were both answered with massive fiscal and monetary rescue, Keynes and Friedman applied simultaneously. The moderns the test names: Joseph Schumpeter — creative destruction, capitalism's gales of innovation destroying old industries to build new ones; Paul Samuelson, the textbook synthesis; Amartya Sen — development as capability, poverty as unfreedom, the thinking behind the Human Development Index; and Paul Krugman, new trade theory — economies of scale explain why similar countries trade similar goods. Recall block. The chain in one breath — who demolished mercantilism, who proved trade always pays, who explained slumps, who explained inflation? Smith; Ricardo; Keynes; Friedman. The paradox of thrift? Everyone saving at once shrinks everyone's income — one person's spending is another's earnings. Stagflation embarrassed whom and vindicated whom? Embarrassed Keynesians; vindicated Friedman. Creative destruction belongs to? Schumpeter. The policy families — fiscal, monetary, supply-side — run through whom? Keynes and Congress; Friedman and the Fed; Laffer and Reagan. Next: the machinery itself — supply, demand, GDP, inflation, and what the Fed actually does. Thanks for listening.
