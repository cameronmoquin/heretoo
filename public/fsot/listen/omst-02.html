OMST episode two: situational judgment, and the method that turns a guess into a decision. Review first; answer out loud. What are the three sections of the OMST? English expression, reading comprehension, situational judgment. How many attempts do you get in twelve months? One. Which section did the officer test drop in October 2025 and the OMST keep? Situational judgment. Three questions for this episode. One: how many answers does a situational judgment item want, and in what order should you give them? Two: what are the four questions that separate the options, and which of them decides the most items? Three: when two options are both defensible, what makes one of them the best? Here we go. Question one, and it changes how you work every item. A situational judgment item gives you a workplace scene and a set of courses of action, and it wants two answers, not one. State's Foreign Service Specialist Assessment Information Guide describes the section as a multiple-choice test asking candidates to supply the best answer choice and the worst answer choice from the options presented. The retired officer-test version ran the same way, and its practice instructions give the mechanic: each scenario and set of options is presented twice; the first time you indicate the best action, the second time the worst. Two selections, two passes over the same options. One sourced line outranks every heuristic in this episode. State's own practice instructions say that knowledge about the Department's policies, procedures, or organizational culture is not required to answer these questions. The embassy is scenery. When an item turns on a rule, the rule is stated inside the scenario, so studying Department procedure for this section buys you nothing. Read the items as ordinary problems between working adults. Question two begins with the order you answer in. Take the worst pick first. Why? A clearly wrong answer is easier to recognise than a best answer competing with a merely good one. On the best pass you are often separating two defensible options, which is slow. On the worst pass you are usually looking for the one option out of step with the other three. Settle that, and the field for the harder pick is shorter by one. The worst pass also rewards a specific expectation. Across forty keyed practice items, the worst answer worked around the problem instead of acting on it seventeen times; it was disproportionate to what the candidate actually knew twelve times; it went to the wrong person seven times; and it broke a rule four times. That ordering is the reverse of what candidates expect. The answer that does nothing is the most common worst answer, and the rule-breaker is the rarest. Why? A rule-breaker announces itself, so it is a weak trap. An option that waits, hedges, or hands the problem sideways sounds careful, and careful is what most people reach for under a clock. So: the four questions, in this order, on both passes. First, does the option break a rule? If the scenario states a policy, a clearance requirement, or a procedure, an option that goes around it is finished, and no detail rescues it. The deadline is real, the colleague is cleared, the requester is senior, the gift arrives after the work is done. None of that touches the rule. This question fires on the fewest items, and when it fires it is decisive, so you clear it first. Second, does it act on the problem, or work around it? Acting means the problem is smaller afterwards. Working around it means the problem is still there and now it is also later. Waiting, saying nothing, routing it onward without adding anything, working harder instead of warning anyone, addressing the awkwardness instead of the lapse. Same failure, different clothes, and by the count you just heard this is the most productive question you can ask. Third, does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it? Not the most senior person, not the person you report to by reflex, and not nobody. Nearest, and capable. An option that jumps three levels for something inside your own authority fails this, and so does one that tells a colleague's supervisor before the colleague. When the decision genuinely belongs to a principal, that principal is the nearest person who can fix it, and your job is to inform and recommend rather than choose. Fourth, is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect? Scenarios hand you a partial picture on purpose. An option that treats a suspicion as established, or reports a colleague for something you have not yet asked them about, overshoots. An option that treats a stated rule as a vague concern undershoots. Proportion is what separates two options that both act, both hold the rules, and both go to the right person. Midpoint check. Which pick do you make first, and what are the four questions in order? Worst first. Does it break a rule. Does it act on the problem, or work around it. Does it go to the nearest person who can actually fix it. Is it proportionate to what you know rather than what you suspect. Question three, the ordinary case: two options survive all four. Watch it on a scenario State has published. A friend asks you to approve a non-standard procurement quickly, and policy says it needs a fuller evaluation. Expediting it as a favour breaks the rule outright, which settles the worst pick before anything else is weighed. Two options hold the rule: telling the officer that friendship aside you follow policy, and telling him you will move as fast as you can but it still gets vetted. Both are defensible. The second also keeps the working relationship, and that is usually what separates a correct answer from the best one. Carry that pattern. Where two options are equally compliant, the best pick is the one that does more of the job at once, which usually means two clauses rather than one. Act and inform. Decline, and offer the compliant route. Assess, and present options. If one option acts but tells no one and another tells someone but does nothing, the one that does both is stronger, and the one that does neither is your worst pick. One discipline for the two passes. The same scenario and the same options come back, and the second pass is where careless points go. Read which pick is being asked for before you look at the options again, and do not let your worst answer default to whichever leftover you liked least. The worst pick is a judgement of its own, made against the four questions. Recall block. How many answers does each scenario want, and in what order do you give them? Two: the best action and the worst action, asked as two passes, and you take the worst first. Name the four questions. Does it break a rule; does it act on the problem or work around it; does it go to the nearest person who can fix it; is it proportionate to what you know. Which of the four catches the most worst answers? The second. The answer that does nothing is the most common worst answer, and the rule-breaker is the rarest. What knowledge is not required for this section? The Department's policies, procedures, or organizational culture; when a rule matters, the scenario states it. Next episode: twelve scenarios, answered out loud, both picks on each, with the four questions running underneath. Thanks for listening.
