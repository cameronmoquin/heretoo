Transcript

Logic drill one: the four questions. This episode works differently from the history ones. I'll read you short arguments, twice each, then ask one question and go silent. Answer out loud before I do; producing the answer yourself is what builds the skill, and saying it aloud keeps you honest. The test itself is read on a screen, so what we're training here is the part that transfers: hearing the skeleton of an argument instantly, conclusion, evidence, and the unstated bridge between them. First, the map. The FSOT logical reasoning section asks only four things. What must be true, that's inference. What holds the argument together, that's assumption. What's wrong with it, that's flaw. And what would make it stronger or weaker. Four questions. Before any argument, know which one you're being asked, because each has a different standard of proof. An inference must be true, not just plausible. An assumption is found by negating: deny the candidate, and if the argument dies, that was the assumption. A flaw should be named, like a species: post hoc, ad hominem, hasty generalization. Strengthen and weaken usually turn on alternative causes. Ready. Argument one. If the ceasefire holds, aid convoys will resume. Aid convoys have not resumed. Again: if the ceasefire holds, aid convoys will resume. Aid convoys have not resumed. What must be true? Say it out loud. The ceasefire has not held. This is modus tollens, the valid one: if A then B, not B, therefore not A. The consequence didn't happen, so the condition can't have either. Notice what you cannot conclude: not that the ceasefire will collapse, not that anyone is blocking aid. Those are plausible; inference demands must. If it needs one extra assumption, it is not an inference. Argument two. Only candidates who pass the FSOT are invited to the orals. Priya was invited to the orals. Again: only candidates who pass the FSOT are invited. Priya was invited. What follows? Out loud. Priya passed the FSOT. The word doing the work is only: it makes passing a necessary condition for invitation. She was invited, so she must have met the requirement. But flip it: if I told you Priya passed, could you conclude she was invited? No. Necessary is not sufficient. Passing is required for invitation, not a guarantee of it. That necessary-sufficient flip is the single most tested confusion in the section. Argument three. Officers who take language training are promoted faster. Therefore the department should require language training for everyone. Again: officers who take language training are promoted faster, so the department should require it. What is this argument assuming? Out loud. That the training causes the faster promotions, rather than just accompanying them. Run the negation test: suppose training does not cause faster promotion, suppose instead that ambitious officers, already on the fast track, are simply the ones who sign up. Then requiring it for everyone achieves nothing, and the argument is dead. When negating a statement kills the argument, you've found the assumption. Correlation offered, causation concluded: the gap between those two is where the assumption always lives. Argument four. The mission's reporting is unreliable, because two cables last year contained errors. Again: the reporting is unreliable, two cables last year had errors. What's the flaw? Name it. Hasty generalization, and specifically a missing denominator. Two errors, out of how many cables? If the mission sent nine hundred, that's an error rate of about a fifth of one percent, which most institutions would frame and hang on the wall. A numerator without a denominator is a number wearing a costume. Any argument that judges a rate from a count alone is bluffing. Argument five. Country X's growth slowed after it raised the minimum wage. Clearly, the wage increase caused the slowdown. Again: growth slowed after the wage increase, so the wage increase caused the slowdown. Name the flaw. Out loud. Post hoc ergo propter hoc: after this, therefore because of this. Sequence in time is the only evidence offered, and time order alone never establishes cause. And now you know the counter-move, because it's the same one every time: to weaken any causal claim, produce an alternative cause. Maybe oil prices fell; maybe a drought hit; maybe growth was already slowing. To strengthen it, do the reverse: rule alternatives out, or find a comparison, a similar country, no wage increase, no slowdown. Cause questions are won and lost on alternative causes. Argument six, the last, and now you do the whole anatomy. Sanctions have cut the regime's oil revenue in half. Its military spending, however, is unchanged. The regime must therefore be drawing on reserves or other income. Once more: sanctions cut oil revenue in half; military spending is unchanged; so the regime must be drawing on reserves or other income. First: which sentence is the conclusion? The last one, flagged by therefore: the regime is drawing on reserves or other income. The other two sentences are the evidence. Second: is the reasoning sound? Nearly. Money must come from somewhere, so if spending held while revenue halved, something fills the gap. The quiet assumptions: that the spending figures are real, and that reserves and other income are the only options. Modest assumptions, but on the test, or in a cable, you'd name them before you signed it. That's the drill. The six moves you just made: modus tollens, only means necessary, negate to find the assumption, demand the denominator, after isn't because, and therefore marks the conclusion. Next drill, the named flaws get a full parade: composition, division, false dilemma, appeal to ignorance, circularity, and the parallel-flaw question, where a treaty and a housecat can commit the same crime. Thanks for listening.
