OMST, The Dress Rehearsal

Transcript

OMST episode nine, the last one: the dress rehearsal, and the plan for the days you have left. Review first; out loud. What category outranks urgent work? Safety and security. Considered is not the same as? Approved. The two questions every situational item asks? Who owns this decision, and how fast does it need to move. This episode has one job: to convert what you know into what you can do at five in the afternoon on a Monday in a test center. Three questions. One: what does a real dress rehearsal involve, and why does the timing of it matter? Two: how should the remaining weeks be divided? Three: what do you do in the last forty-eight hours, and in the room itself? Here we go. Question one: the rehearsal. A practice section taken at a comfortable hour, in a comfortable chair, with the phone nearby, is not practice. It is study with a timer on. The rehearsal that matters replicates four things. The hour: your appointment is late afternoon, which for most people is the low point of the day, so at least twice you should sit a full-length paper starting at that hour and find out what your late-afternoon attention actually does. The duration: sit the whole thing without stopping, because the fatigue in the last section is the thing you are training for and it is precisely what a twenty-minute practice block never shows you. The conditions: no phone, no notes, no pausing, nothing to drink that you would not have in a test center. And the sequence: sections back to back. Do that twice, not five times. Full rehearsals are expensive and their value is calibration, not score. Two of them tell you what you need to know: where your attention drops, whether you are running out of time in a section, and which of the three sections is actually weakest when you are tired rather than fresh. Which brings the scoring habit that matters more than the score itself. After every rehearsal, sort your wrong answers into exactly three piles. Pile one: I did not know it. That is content, and it is the easiest to fix. Pile two: I knew it and misread the question. That is the stem problem, and it is fixed by slowing down on the first line, not by studying. Pile three: I got it down to two and chose wrong. That is the most valuable pile you have, because those are the items where a rule of thumb would have decided it — the more modest option in reading comprehension, the act-and-inform option in situational judgment. Count the piles. Most people find pile two is bigger than they expect, and it is the cheapest one to shrink. Midpoint check. Why two full rehearsals rather than five? Their value is calibration, not score; two is enough to find where attention drops, and the time is better spent on the wrong answers than on more full sittings. Question two: dividing the time. Work backward from the nineteenth. The first stretch is acquisition: the six method episodes, the English episodes, and untimed question practice where you read every explanation, including the ones you got right. Getting an item right for the wrong reason is invisible unless you read the explanation, and situational judgment is full of items you can get right by instinct once and wrong by instinct twice. The middle stretch is timed work, section by section rather than full papers. Twenty-four situational items in thirty minutes. Twenty reading items in thirty. English under time, where the goal is speed, because English is where you bank the minutes you will spend on passages. Alternate the sections rather than blocking them; interleaving practice produces worse practice sessions and better test performance, which is one of the most consistently replicated findings in the study of learning and one almost nobody follows, because blocked practice feels better while you are doing it. The last stretch, roughly the final week, is rehearsal and review only. Two full papers at the real hour, the wrong answers sorted into the three piles, and the method episodes replayed once each. Learn nothing new in the last week. Anything you meet for the first time in the final days will be shaky under pressure and will crowd out something you already had. And through all of it, spacing. Four half-hour sessions across four days beat one four-hour session, by a wide margin, for anything you need to still have a week later. This is not a productivity opinion; it is the most reliable finding in the memory literature, and the one that study plans most often ignore. Question three: the last forty-eight hours and the room. Two days out, do the practical work so it is not in your head: confirm the appointment time and the test center address, work out the trip at that hour of day rather than at noon, and lay out the identification exactly as the confirmation specifies. The day before, one light pass over the method — the six situational steps, the five archetypes, the six reading steps, the four traps — and then stop. Sleep is a study technique; the night before a test it outperforms another hour of review, because memory consolidates in sleep and fatigue costs you the stem-misreading errors from pile two. In the room. Read every stem twice; it is the single highest-return habit you have. Bank time on English so you can spend it in the passages. In reading, stay inside the text and take the more modest option when two survive. In situational judgment, run the method rather than your gut, and resist the urge to revisit: your considered second guess on a judgment item is usually a rationalization dressed as care. Answer every question, because unanswered is wrong with certainty and a guess after eliminating one option is strictly better. And if a section goes badly, put it down when it ends; the next section is scored separately and carrying the last one into it is how one bad stretch becomes three. One closing thought about the test itself. Every scenario on that situational section is a small version of the job, and the job is holding other people's time, words, and access with enough judgment that nobody has to check your work. When you are genuinely torn between two options, ask which one a person doing that job well would choose — not the most agreeable, not the most cautious, but the one that protects the principal's time, the accuracy of the record, and the security of the post. That question has decided nearly every item in this course, and it will decide most of the items on your test. Final recall. The three piles? Did not know it; misread the stem; got it to two and chose wrong. Blocked or interleaved practice? Interleaved: worse sessions, better test. What do you do in the last week? Rehearse and review only; learn nothing new. Two options survive in reading comprehension — which do you take? The more modest one. And the question that decides a hard situational item? What would someone doing this job well do: protect the principal's time, the accuracy of the record, the security of the post. That is the whole course. One attempt, one afternoon, and you have a method for every section of it. Good luck on the nineteenth. Thanks for listening.
