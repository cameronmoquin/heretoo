The World Before 1789

Transcript

Episode fifteen: the world before 1789 — the spine every other date hangs on. Review; out loud. The IMF treats what? Currency and balance-of-payments crises. An embassy is inviolable — meaning? The host may not enter without permission; it is not the sender's territory. Which needs two-thirds of the Senate — treaty or executive agreement? Treaty. Three questions. One: what did the Peace of Westphalia invent that diplomats still live inside? Two: why did Europe, a backwater in 1400, run the world by 1800? Three: which ideas from the Enlightenment are sitting inside the American founding documents? The method for this episode: anchors. One event per era, held hard, so any question can be placed relative to something you know. Anchor one: 1215, Magna Carta. English barons force King John to concede that even the king is under law. It's a feudal document about baronial privileges, but it becomes the myth and the seed: due process, no taxation without consent. Every later constitutionalism cites it. Anchor two: 1453, Constantinople falls to the Ottomans under Mehmed the Second. The Byzantine Empire — Rome's thousand-year eastern continuation — ends; the Ottomans bestride the crossroads of Europe and Asia for the next four centuries. Overland routes east now pass through Ottoman hands, which is one spur for anchor three: 1492, Columbus sails west for Spain, and 1494, the Treaty of Tordesillas — the Pope draws a line splitting the non-European world between Spain and Portugal. Which is why Brazil speaks Portuguese and the rest of Latin America speaks Spanish: the line, nothing else. The Columbian Exchange follows — crops, animals, and diseases crossing the Atlantic both ways; Old World smallpox kills perhaps ninety percent of the native population of the Americas, the deadliest event in human history by proportion. Anchor four: 1517, Martin Luther's Ninety-Five Theses. The Reformation splits Western Christendom — Lutherans, then Calvinists, then England's separate exit under Henry the Eighth. A century of religious war follows, climaxing in the Thirty Years' War, 1618 to 1648, which kills perhaps a third of Germany. Exhaustion produces anchor five, and question one's answer: the Peace of Westphalia, 1648. Westphalia's invention is sovereignty — the ruler decides the realm's religion, and states, not popes or emperors, are the units of international life, each supreme inside its borders, legally equal outside them. When diplomats today speak of the Westphalian system, sovereign equality, or non-interference in internal affairs, they are quoting 1648. The entire architecture from episode fourteen — embassies, treaties, the UN's sovereign-equality clause — is Westphalia's furniture. Midpoint check. Why does Brazil speak Portuguese? The Tordesillas line of 1494 — Brazil's coast lay east of it, in Portugal's half of the world. Question two: Europe's rise. In 1400, the great powers by wealth and sophistication were Ming China and the Islamic empires; China's treasure fleets under Zheng He dwarfed anything European. The standard explanation for the reversal is competition: Europe's fractured map — dozens of rival states — forced constant military and commercial innovation, while unified empires could and did turn inward; China scrapped its fleet by imperial decree. Add gunpowder armies, joint-stock companies like the Dutch and English East India Companies — war and trade run as corporations — the scientific revolution of Copernicus, Galileo, and Newton, and Atlantic silver and sugar wealth built on enslaved African labor: the engine assembles by 1700. The three early modern empires to know besides the Ottomans: Safavid Persia — Shia Islam becomes Persia's identity, the root of Iran's distinctness today — Mughal India, Muslim rulers over a Hindu majority until Britain swallows the subcontinent, and Tokugawa Japan, closed by choice from 1639 until an American squadron under Commodore Perry forces it open in 1853. Meanwhile England runs its constitutional experiment: Civil War in the 1640s, a king beheaded, Cromwell's republic, restoration, and then anchor six — the Glorious Revolution, 1688: Parliament removes one king and hires another, and the 1689 English Bill of Rights makes the bargain explicit — no taxation, no standing army, no suspending of laws without Parliament. William and Mary reign because Parliament says so. A century later, thirteen colonies full of Englishmen will insist on exactly these rights, which is question three's bridge. The Enlightenment, the seventeen hundreds' project of applying reason to government, and the test asks it as matching pairs — thinker to idea. John Locke: natural rights — life, liberty, property — and government by consent, dissolvable when it breaks the social contract; Jefferson's Declaration is Locke with the serial numbers barely filed. Montesquieu: separation of powers into legislative, executive, and judicial — Madison's floor plan. Rousseau: the social contract and popular sovereignty. Voltaire: religious toleration and free expression. Adam Smith, 1776: the market's invisible hand — the same year as the Declaration, a pairing the test loves. Hobbes, the dark precursor: life in the state of nature is nasty, brutish, and short, so men trade liberty for order — the case for strong government the others argue against. So question three: the Declaration is Locke, the Constitution is Montesquieu, the Bill of Rights is 1689 with amendments numbered. The American founding is the Enlightenment shipped across an ocean and ratified. Recall block. Westphalia's invention, one word? Sovereignty — states supreme inside their borders, equal outside. Order these: Luther, Magna Carta, Constantinople's fall, Glorious Revolution. Magna Carta 1215, Constantinople 1453, Luther 1517, Glorious Revolution 1688. Locke and Montesquieu — which document does each haunt? Locke the Declaration; Montesquieu the Constitution. What forced Japan open, and when? Perry's American squadron, 1853. 1776 published what twin landmarks? The Declaration of Independence and The Wealth of Nations. Next: the age of revolutions — France, Napoleon, 1848, and the empires that carved the world. Thanks for listening.
