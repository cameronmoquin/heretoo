Logic Bootcamp, Evidence at Full Strength

Logical Reasoning · 7:05 · Episode 43

Open the full player

Transcript

Logic bootcamp three: strengthen and weaken at full difficulty — competing hypotheses, and the evidence that separates them. Recap; out loud. The overlap minimum? Add percents, subtract one hundred. Cited means? Said, not proved. Today's frame upgrades the causal toolkit: every strengthen-weaken item is secretly a contest between the argument's story and its best rival. Your job is to find the evidence that discriminates — that fits one story and embarrasses the other. Evidence that fits both stories equally moves nothing, however dramatic it sounds. Argument twice, question, silence, out loud. One. Posts that hold weekly security briefings report fewer incidents than posts that don't. The briefings evidently prevent incidents. Again: weekly-briefing posts report fewer incidents; briefings prevent incidents. The rival story first — name it. Calmer posts have time for briefings — safety causes briefings, not the reverse. Or the darker rival: report fewer — maybe briefing-heavy posts undercount. Now, which evidence discriminates: A, briefings follow a standard curriculum; B, when high-incident posts adopted briefings, incidents subsequently fell while comparable posts stayed flat? B — time order plus control fits the briefing story and embarrasses the reverse-causation story. A is true-sounding filler: a standard curriculum fits both stories equally. Filler that fits both is the section's favorite wrong answer. Two. A columnist argues: the literacy program failed — test scores in participating schools haven't risen in three years. Again: scores flat for three years in participating schools; program failed. Weaken it with the strongest single fact. The counterfactual: comparable non-participating schools' scores fell over the same period. Flat versus falling is success wearing failure's clothes. The general principle — judge policies against the counterfactual, not against the past: what would have happened without it is the honest baseline, and it's invisible unless a comparison group supplies it. This is the single most transferable idea in the bootcamps: from aid programs to sanctions, effect equals outcome minus counterfactual, never outcome minus yesterday. Three. Hospitals that treat the most gunshot patients have the highest gunshot mortality rates. Avoid those hospitals if shot. Again: highest-volume gunshot hospitals, highest mortality; avoid them. The rival, and the discriminating evidence? Rival: the worst cases go to the trauma centers — severity selects the hospital, the hospital doesn't produce the death. Discriminating evidence: mortality adjusted for wound severity — if the high-volume centers do better on like-for-like cases, the advice inverts. Name the pattern: confounding by severity, the medical cousin of selection effect; adjusting for it is what case-mix and controlling for mean. When groups differ at intake, raw rates mislead at discharge. Four. Trade openness causes peace, says the theorist: no two countries with McDonald's have fought a war. Again: trade openness causes peace; no two McDonald's countries have warred. Weaken. Two blades. The confound: McDonald's tracks wealth and Western alignment — rich aligned democracies weren't fighting anyway; the arches mark the peace, they don't make it. And the counterexample fatal to the slogan's letter: post-2014 Russia and Ukraine — both had McDonald's; the golden arches theory died at the border it was coined to celebrate. A cute correlation with a confound and a counterexample is a slogan, not a law. Five. The minister claims the new port will pay for itself: traffic projections show volume tripling within a decade. Again: the port will pay for itself; projections show tripling volume. What single question most exposes this argument? Whose projections, built on what? — the source and assumptions question. Projections are conclusions wearing evidence costumes: if the builder's consultants produced them assuming the port exists and thrives, the argument is circular — the forecast assumes the success it's cited to prove. Strengthen would be independent projections, or comparable ports' realized histories. Rule: a forecast is only as strong as its independence from the conclusion it serves. Six, full anatomy, you alone. Analysts note that countries adopting the new banking standards attract more foreign investment. A senator concludes America should adopt them to attract investment. Again: adopters attract more investment; therefore adopt to attract. Run the full protocol out loud: classify the evidence, name two rivals, name the discriminating evidence, and state what the conclusion additionally assumes even if causation holds. Evidence: cross-country correlation. Rivals: attractive economies adopt standards — reverse causation; and institutional quality drives both — common cause. Discriminating evidence: investment rising after adoption, against matched non-adopters. The extra assumption: what worked there transfers here — external validity; America's context may already price in what the standards signal elsewhere. Four moves, one breath. That's the full protocol, and it is the hardest thing this section asks. The residue. Evidence that fits both stories? Moves nothing — filler. The honest baseline? The counterfactual — comparison, not yesterday. Raw rates mislead when? Groups differ at intake — adjust for severity, for selection. A forecast's strength? Its independence from the conclusion it serves. The transfer assumption? External validity — there is not here. Next: the final gauntlet — thirty seconds a question, everything mixed. Thanks for listening.
